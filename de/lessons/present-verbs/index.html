Regular Verbs & Word Order (verb-second)

What you'll learn

  • Conjugate regular (weak) verbs in the present tense with the endings -e, -st, -t, -en.
  • Handle spelling adjustments like du arbeitest and du heißt.
  • Talk about daily activities (wohnen, spielen, machen, lernen, arbeiten).
  • Apply the verb-second (V2) rule so the conjugated verb stays in position two.
  • Build sentences that begin with a time or place word, e.g. Heute spiele ich Fußball.

Dialogue

Lena

Wo wohnst du, Tom?

Where do you live, Tom?

Tom

Ich wohne in Berlin. Und du?

I live in Berlin. And you?

Lena

Ich wohne in Hamburg. Was machst du heute?

I live in Hamburg. What are you doing today?

Tom

Heute spiele ich Fußball mit Max.

Today I'm playing football with Max.

Lena

Spielst du oft Fußball?

Do you play football often?

Tom

Ja, sehr gern. Und am Abend lerne ich Deutsch.

Yes, very gladly. And in the evening I study German.

Lena

Ich lerne auch Deutsch! Wir lernen zusammen, oder?

I'm learning German too! We'll study together, right?

Tom

Gute Idee. Hörst du gern Musik beim Lernen?

Good idea. Do you like listening to music while studying?

Lena

Ja, ich höre immer Musik. Was arbeitest du eigentlich?

Yes, I always listen to music. What do you actually do for work?

Tom

Ich arbeite in einem Café. Heute koche ich aber zu Hause.

I work in a café. But today I'm cooking at home.

Lena

Schön! Dann lernen wir morgen zusammen.

Nice! Then we'll study together tomorrow.

Grammar

Regular (weak) verbs in the present

Most German verbs are regular: you take the stem (the infinitive minus -en) and add a personal ending. Once you know these four endings you can use thousands of verbs.

German has only one present tense, so ich spiele covers "I play", "I am playing" and "I do play".

Small spelling adjustments

Two groups need a tiny tweak so they can be pronounced:

  • If the stem ends in -t or -d (like arbeiten, finden), add an extra e before the ending in the du / er / ihr forms: du arbeitest, er arbeitet, ihr arbeitet.
  • If the stem already ends in an s-sound (-s, -ß, -z, -x, like heißen), the du form adds only -t, not -st: du heißt (not heißst).

The verb-second (V2) rule

This is the single most important word-order rule in German: in a normal statement, the conjugated verb is always the second element in the sentence. "Element" means a whole chunk (a subject, a time phrase, a place phrase), not a single word.

When the sentence starts with the subject, this looks just like English: Ich spiele heute Fußball. But you can move another element to the front for emphasis — and then the subject must jump to after the verb, so the verb stays in position two:

Position 1Position 2 (verb)Rest
Ichspieleheute Fußball.
Heutespieleich Fußball.
In Berlinwohnter.

Notice that Heute ich spiele… is wrong — that would put the verb in position three. The rule is: front whatever you like, but keep the verb second and let the subject follow it.

Pronunciation tip

The -en ending on infinitives and the wir/sie forms is usually swallowed in speech, sounding like a quick -uhn with very little vowel: wohnen ≈ "VOH-n'n", spielen ≈ "SHPEE-l'n". Don't over-pronounce that final e. And remember w = English v: wohnen starts like "vo-".

Culture note

The verb-second rule gives German its characteristic flexibility: because the verb is anchored in position two, you can move time and place phrases to the front to set the scene without ever losing the listener. This is why German speakers so often begin sentences with Heute…, Morgen…, In Berlin… — the front slot is prime real estate for whatever the speaker wants to emphasise.

The little word gern (or gerne) has no neat English equivalent. Placed after a verb, it turns "I do X" into "I like doing X": Ich spiele gern Fußball means "I like playing football". It is one of the most useful words at A1 because it lets you express preferences without needing a separate verb like "to like". Germans use it constantly, also as a warm reply to thanks or requests — Gern! means "My pleasure!".
