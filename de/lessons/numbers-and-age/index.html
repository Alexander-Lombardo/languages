Dialogue

Anna

Hallo Tom! Wie alt bist du?

Hi Tom! How old are you?

Tom

Ich bin einundzwanzig Jahre alt. Und du?

I'm twenty-one years old. And you?

Anna

Ich bin neunzehn. Mein Bruder ist auch einundzwanzig.

I'm nineteen. My brother is also twenty-one.

Tom

Wie viele Geschwister hast du?

How many siblings do you have?

Anna

Zwei. Ein Bruder und eine Schwester.

Two. A brother and a sister.

Tom

Schön. Hast du meine Telefonnummer?

Nice. Do you have my phone number?

Anna

Nein. Wie ist deine Nummer?

No. What's your number?

Tom

Null-eins-sieben-vier, drei-zwei-acht, neun-fünf.

One moment… zero, one, seven, four… okay, I've got it!

Tom

Super. Bis bald, Anna!

Great. See you soon, Anna!

From 13 to 19 you just add -zehn (ten) to the unit, with two small spelling changes: sechzehn (not sechszehn) and siebzehn (not siebenzehn).

The tens and the famous reversal

The tens end in -zig (except dreißig, spelled with ß): zwanzig, dreißig, vierzig, fünfzig, sechzig, siebzig, achtzig, neunzig.

Now the part that surprises everyone: for numbers between the tens, German says the units first, joined by und ("and"), like the old English "four-and-twenty". So 21 is literally one-and-twenty:

Write them as one long word, with no spaces. Note that the -s of eins drops: it's einundzwanzig, not eins-. The top of the range is hundert (100), often written einhundert.

Age, phone numbers and "how much?"

To ask age, use the verb sein: Wie alt bist du? (informal) or Wie alt sind Sie? (formal). Answer with Ich bin … Jahre alt — and in casual speech you can simply say Ich bin 21, dropping Jahre alt.

Phone numbers are normally read digit by digit (or in pairs), not as one big number: 0174 32 89 → null eins sieben vier, drei zwei, acht neun.

Finally, two question words for quantity: Wie viel? (how much — for uncountable things and money) and Wie viele? (how many — for countable things): Wie viel kostet das? vs. Wie viele Geschwister hast du?

Pronunciation tip

Two number traps for English speakers: zwei starts with the ts sound ("tsvy"), and on the phone Germans often say zwo instead of zwei so it isn't confused with drei. And ei is always "eye": eins, zwei, drei, dreißig all rhyme with English "eye", never "ee".

Culture note

The "units-first" way of saying numbers (einundzwanzig = one-and-twenty) is more than a curiosity — it genuinely trips up learners when they hear a number like vierundsiebzig and have to mentally flip it. Even native speakers occasionally transpose digits when writing down a spoken number, a known source of small errors in banking and on the phone; there's even a German campaign (the "Zwanzigeins" initiative) that has argued for saying numbers in the spoken-left-to-right order, though tradition has held.

On the phone, Germans commonly read numbers in pairs and say zwo for zwei to avoid confusion with drei. Age, by contrast, is treated fairly openly in casual settings — asking Wie alt bist du? among friends or classmates is normal, though with new adult acquaintances in a formal context it's still more polite to let the topic come up on its own.
