Greetings & Introductions

What you'll learn

  • Greet people by the time of day and say goodbye, formally and informally.
  • Choose correctly between the informal du and the formal Sie for "you".
  • Introduce yourself and ask someone's name with Wie heißt du? / Wie heißen Sie?
  • Ask how someone is and reply: gut, schlecht, es geht.
  • Use the basic courtesy phrases that frame every conversation.

Dialogue

Lena

Hallo! Ich heiße Lena. Wie heißt du?

Hi! My name is Lena. What's your name?

Tom

Hallo Lena! Ich heiße Tom. Freut mich.

Hi Lena! My name is Tom. Nice to meet you.

Lena

Freut mich auch. Wie geht's?

Nice to meet you too. How are you?

Tom

Gut, danke! Und dir?

Good, thanks! And you?

Lena

Tom

Grammar

du or Sie? The two words for "you"

German has two ways to say you to one person, and choosing the right one is the first social skill you need. du is informal — for friends, family, children, classmates and (increasingly) young people your own age. Sie is formal and polite — for strangers, older people, shopkeepers, officials and people at work. The safe default with an adult you don't know is Sie; you wait to be invited to switch to du.

The two forms change the words around them, so a whole question shifts:

Informal (du)Formal (Sie)English
Wie heißt du?Wie heißen Sie?What's your name?
Wie geht's?Wie geht es Ihnen?How are you?
Und dir?Und Ihnen?And you?

Note that Sie (you, polite) is always capitalised — that is how you tell it apart from sie (she/they), which you meet in the next lesson.

Greeting by the time of day

Germans pick a greeting to match the clock. Hallo works any time and is friendly; the guten… greetings are a touch more polite.

  • Guten Morgen — in the morning, roughly until 10–11 a.m.
  • Guten Tag — through the day; the all-purpose polite hello.
  • Guten Abend — in the evening.
  • Gute Nacht — only when going to bed, not as a hello.

To say goodbye, use tschüss (casual) or auf Wiedersehen (formal), and bis bald for "see you soon".

Introducing yourself and asking how someone is

To give your name, say Ich heiße + your name, or Ich bin + your name (e.g. Ich heiße Anna / Ich bin Anna). When you meet someone, the standard reply is Freut mich ("pleased to meet you").

To ask how things are, the answer comes from a small set of words:

ReplyMeaning
(sehr) gut(very) good / well
es gehtso-so, okay
nicht so gutnot so good
schlechtbad

Then bounce the question back with Und dir? (informal) or Und Ihnen? (formal).

Pronunciation tip

The ei and ie pairs are easy to mix up. Say the name of the second letter: ei sounds like English "eye" (heiße, Wein, mein), while ie sounds like "ee" (Sie, wie, vier). So heißen is "HY-sen" but Sie is "zee". And remember the ß in heiße is just a sharp s.

Culture note

Whether you say du or Sie is a genuine social decision in German, not just a grammar rule. Among adults who don't know each other, Sie is the respectful default, and switching to du is often a small ceremony — the older or more senior person traditionally offers it ("Wir können uns duzen"). Get it wrong and you can sound either cold or over-familiar, so when in doubt, start with Sie.

Greetings also vary by region. Across most of Germany you'll hear Hallo and Guten Tag, but in Bavaria and Austria the usual greeting is Grüß Gott, in Switzerland Grüezi, and in the north you may catch a brisk Moin at any hour of the day. Replying in kind is a friendly signal that you've noticed where you are.
