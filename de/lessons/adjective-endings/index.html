Adjective Endings

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Understand why an adjective before a noun needs an ending while one after sein/werden does not.
  • Apply the WEAK endings (-e / -en) after a definite article (der, diese, jeder).
  • Apply the MIXED endings after ein, kein and the possessives, which show gender in the masculine and neuter nominative.
  • Apply the STRONG endings (no article) where the adjective itself carries the article ending.
  • Decline adjectives correctly in the nominative, accusative and dative.

Yes, I like the big balcony. And the bright kitchen is great.

With a new sofa the cosy living room would be perfect.

Grammar

The big idea: only adjectives before a noun get an ending

An adjective after sein or werden never changes: Der Wein ist gut. Die Suppe ist warm. But the moment the adjective stands in front of the noun, it must take an ending that agrees with the noun's gender, case and number: der gute Wein, die warme Suppe. Which ending you use depends entirely on what comes before the adjective — and there are exactly three situations.

1. WEAK endings — after a definite article

After der, die, das, die (pl.) and the der-words dieser, jeder, welcher, alle, the article already shows the gender and case. The adjective can relax: it only ever takes -e or -en. The simple rule of thumb: -e in the nominative singular (and the neuter/feminine accusative), -en everywhere else.

 masc.neut.fem.plural
Nom.der gute Weindas kalte Wasserdie warme Suppedie guten Weine
Acc.den guten Weindas kalte Wasserdie warme Suppedie guten Weine
Dat.dem guten Weindem kalten Wasserder warmen Suppeden guten Weinen

Notice how only the top-left corner of the table is full of -e; the masculine accusative and the whole dative row are already -en.

2. MIXED endings — after ein, kein and the possessives

After ein, kein, mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser, euer, there is a problem: ein has no ending in three spots — masculine nominative (ein), neuter nominative (ein) and neuter accusative (ein). In exactly those spots the adjective has to step in and show the gender. Everywhere else the endings are the same as the weak pattern (-en, plus -e for the feminine).

 masc.neut.fem.plural
Nom.ein guter Weinein kaltes Wassereine warme Suppemeine guten Weine
Acc.einen guten Weinein kaltes Wassereine warme Suppemeine guten Weine
Dat.einem guten Weineinem kalten Wassereiner warmen Suppemeinen guten Weinen

So the three „rescue“ endings to memorise are -er (ein guter Mann), -es (ein gutes Kind) and again -es for the neuter accusative. Plural possessives behave like the weak plural (always -en).

3. STRONG endings — when there is no article at all

With no article in front (often in the plural, or with uncountable nouns), nothing else is marking the case — so the adjective itself takes the ending the article would have had: guter Wein (like der), kaltes Wasser (like das), warme Suppe (like die).

 masc.neut.fem.plural
Nom.guter Weinkaltes Wasserwarme Suppegute Weine
Acc.guten Weinkaltes Wasserwarme Suppegute Weine
Dat.gutem Weinkaltem Wasserwarmer Suppeguten Weinen

The one exception to „copy the article ending“ is the masculine and neuter dative, where the adjective takes -em (the -s of des is saved for the noun, which already adds -s in the genitive). Otherwise: if you know der/die/das/den/dem, you already know the strong endings.

Pronunciation tip

When you read a string like ein guter Wein, don't over-stress the ending — German adjective endings are unstressed and the vowel in -er, -es, -en, -em is a quick, neutral „uh“. The stress stays on the root: GU-ter, WAR-me, KAL-tes. Saying the ending too loudly is a classic learner giveaway.

Reading: Die neue Wohnung

Anna sucht eine neue Wohnung. Die alte Wohnung ist zu klein, und der laute Verkehr vor dem Fenster stört sie jeden Tag. Sie wünscht sich ein helles Zimmer, eine moderne Küche und einen ruhigen Garten.

Gestern hat sie eine schöne Wohnung in einem alten Haus besichtigt. Das große Wohnzimmer hat ihr sofort gefallen. Mit den hohen Fenstern und dem warmen Licht wirkt es sehr gemütlich. Nur die hohe Miete macht ihr Sorgen.

Ihre beste Freundin sagt: „Eine gute Wohnung in einer ruhigen Straße findet man nicht jeden Tag. Nimm sie!“ Anna denkt nach. Vielleicht ist diese teure Wohnung doch die richtige Entscheidung.

Translation

Anna is looking for a new apartment. The old apartment is too small, and the loud traffic in front of the window bothers her every day. She wants a bright room, a modern kitchen and a quiet garden.

Yesterday she viewed a beautiful apartment in an old building. She liked the large living room immediately. With the tall windows and the warm light it feels very cosy. Only the high rent worries her.

Her best friend says: “You don't find a good apartment on a quiet street every day. Take it!” Anna thinks it over. Maybe this expensive apartment is the right decision after all.

Culture note

Renting is the norm in the German-speaking world far more than in many countries: over half of households in Germany rent rather than own, and apartment hunting (Wohnungssuche) in cities like Berlin, Munich or Zürich can be fiercely competitive. Listings come bristling with adjectives — helle, ruhige, sanierte, zentrale Wohnung — and with abbreviations like 2 ZKB (zwei Zimmer, Küche, Bad). You'll often see Kaltmiete (the base rent) versus Warmmiete (rent including heating and utilities), so reading those adjective-laden ads accurately really pays off.

Talk about it

  • Welche der drei Endungsmuster findest du am schwierigsten? — Which of the three ending patterns do you find most difficult?
  • Beschreibe dein Lieblingsessen mit passenden Adjektiven (warm, frisch, scharf …). — Describe your favourite food with suitable adjectives (warm, fresh, spicy …).
  • Würdest du lieber in einem alten Haus oder in einer modernen Wohnung leben? — Would you rather live in an old house or a modern apartment?
