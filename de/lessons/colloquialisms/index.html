Colloquialisms & Umgangssprache

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Recognise and decode casual contractions: haste, gibt's, hamwa, kannste, nix, mal eben.
  • Use spoken fillers (also, ähm, halt, sozusagen, ne?) to sound natural and buy thinking time.
  • Deploy colloquial intensifiers (mega, total, voll, echt, krass) appropriately — and know when not to.
  • Understand common Jugendsprache and where it belongs (and where it doesn't).
  • Switch consciously between Umgangssprache and Standardsprache depending on situation.
  • Map colloquial forms back to their standard equivalents for writing and exams.

Dialogue

Tom

Na, haste am Wochenende was vor?

Hey, you got anything planned for the weekend?

Lena

Ähm, eigentlich nix. Ich hab grad echt kein Bock auf große Pläne, ne?

Um, nothing really. I just honestly don't feel like big plans, you know?

Tom

Verstehe. Wir könnten halt mal eben einen Kaffee trinken gehen, ganz entspannt.

Got it. We could just grab a quick coffee, totally chilled.

Lena

Oh, das klingt mega. Kostet ja auch nicht die Welt — ich hab eh kaum Kohle gerade.

Oh, that sounds great. It's not like it costs the earth either — I'm basically broke right now anyway.

Tom

Kein Stress. Sagen wir so um drei? Passt das?

No worries. Let's say around three? Does that work?

Lena

Voll. Bis dann, und zieh nicht so schicke Klamotten an, sonst fühl ich mich underdressed.

Totally. See you then, and don't wear anything too fancy or I'll feel underdressed.

Tom

Haha, alles klar, kommt im Hoodie. Bis später!

Haha, alright, coming in a hoodie. Later!

Grammar

Contractions in fast speech

Spoken German routinely fuses pronouns onto verbs and clips endings. You won't write these (except in dialogue), but you must hear them. The key is that the standard form is always recoverable:

hastehast dudo you have
kannstekannst ducan you
gibt'sgibt esis there / are there
hamwa / hammwerhaben wirwe have

kein Bockkeine Lustno desire / can't be bothered

Notice how subjects often drop entirely: Wusst ich nicht (= Das wusste ich nicht), Mach ich (= Das mache ich). The verb stays in second-ish position but the obvious subject simply vanishes.

Fillers (Füllwörter / Flickwörter)

Fillers are not laziness — used well, they smooth a conversation and signal stance. Used to excess, they clutter. The C1 goal is a light, natural sprinkling:

  • also — "so / well", starts or organises a thought: Also, ich finde …
  • halt / eben — "just / simply", resignation or matter-of-factness: Das ist halt so.
  • ähm — "um", pure hesitation while you find the word.
  • sozusagen — "so to speak", flags a loose or metaphorical formulation.
  • ne? / oder? / gell? (south) — tag question seeking agreement: Das ist gut, ne?
  • quasi — "basically / sort of", hedges a claim.

Intensifiers and youth language

Casual German loves to amplify. The neutral sehr gives way to a rotating cast of intensifiers, several of which feel young or trendy:

WordForceExample
echtgenuinelyDas war echt gut.
totalcompletelyIch bin total müde.
volltotally (young)Das ist voll nett.
mega / ultrasuper (young)Der Film war mega.
krasswild / intense (young)Krass, oder?

A warning: words like voll, mega and krass mark you as casual (and sometimes as young). They are excellent among friends and badly out of place in a cover letter, a presentation, or an exam essay — there you want sehr, äußerst, überaus, bemerkenswert.

Standardsprache vs. Umgangssprache

The two are not "right" vs. "wrong" — they are different registers for different situations. The mark of a near-native speaker is choosing the level on purpose, not by accident.

UmgangsspracheStandardsprache
Ich hab kein Bock.Ich habe keine Lust.
Der Typ ist voll nett.Der Mann ist sehr freundlich.
Das ist totaler Quatsch.Das ist völliger Unsinn.
Wir hängen einfach ab.Wir verbringen einfach Zeit miteinander.
Hast du das gecheckt?Hast du das verstanden?

Pronunciation tip

Casual German swallows sounds, and that's the whole point. The unstressed -e in ich habe routinely disappears (ich hab), es shrinks to a hissed 's (gibt's, geht's, hat's), and einen collapses to a barely-there 'n ('n Kaffee). Don't over-articulate these in relaxed speech — clinging to every full ending is itself a giveaway that German isn't your first language. Listen for the rhythm, not the spelling.

Reading: Wie redet man eigentlich „richtig“?

Wer Deutsch nur aus dem Lehrbuch kennt, ist oft überrascht, wie wenig die Leute auf der Straße so sprechen, wie es im Buch steht. Niemand sagt im Gespräch ständig „Ich habe es nicht gewusst“ — man sagt eher „Wusst ich nicht“ und verschluckt dabei die halbe Grammatik. Das ist kein schlechtes Deutsch, sondern Umgangssprache: die natürliche, gesprochene Variante, die von Region, Alter und Situation abhängt.

Problematisch wird es erst, wenn die Ebenen durcheinandergeraten. Ein „Voll krass, was der Chef da abgezogen hat“ mag unter Freunden völlig okay sein, in einer Bewerbung oder Hausarbeit wirkt es deplatziert. Die eigentliche Kunst auf hohem Niveau ist also nicht, möglichst gewählt zu reden, sondern das Register bewusst zu wechseln — locker mit Freunden, präzise im Beruf, korrekt auf dem Papier.

Füllwörter wie „halt“, „also“ oder „ne?“ sind dabei keine Fehler. Sie geben dem Sprecher Zeit zum Denken und dem Gespräch einen menschlichen Rhythmus. Wer sie nie benutzt, klingt schnell wie ein Roboter.

Translation

Anyone who knows German only from the textbook is often surprised how little people on the street actually talk the way the book says. Nobody constantly says "I did not know it" in conversation — they're more likely to say "Didn't know that," swallowing half the grammar in the process. That isn't bad German, it's colloquial language: the natural, spoken variant that depends on region, age and situation.

It only becomes a problem when the levels get mixed up. A "Totally insane what the boss pulled there" may be perfectly fine among friends, but in a job application or a term paper it comes across as out of place. The real skill at an advanced level, then, isn't to speak as elaborately as possible, but to switch register consciously — relaxed with friends, precise at work, correct on paper.

Fillers like "halt," "also" or "ne?" are not mistakes in this. They give the speaker time to think and the conversation a human rhythm. Anyone who never uses them quickly ends up sounding like a robot.

Culture note

German is famously plurizentrisch and stratified: there is the formal Standarddeutsch of news broadcasts and contracts, a relaxed everyday Umgangssprache, and beneath that the regional dialects (Dialekte) and city slangs. Most people glide between these without thinking — a Berliner might say icke for ich at the kiosk and flawless Hochdeutsch in a meeting an hour later. Mastering this gliding, rather than any one level, is what makes speech sound native.

Jugendsprache changes fast and is even tracked publicly: since 2008 the Langenscheidt publisher and now a youth jury have crowned a yearly Jugendwort des Jahres (Youth Word of the Year) — past winners include cringe, lost, Smombie (smartphone-zombie) and goofy. The very fact that such words date quickly is the lesson: slang signals belonging to a moment and a group, so a learner should wield it lightly and always keep the standard register in reserve.

Talk about it

  • In welchen Situationen benutzen Sie Umgangssprache, in welchen lieber die Standardsprache? — In which situations do you use colloquial language, and in which do you prefer the standard?
  • Gibt es in Ihrer Muttersprache ähnliche Füllwörter wie „halt“ oder „ne?“ — Are there fillers in your native language similar to "halt" or "ne?"
  • Finden Sie Jugendsprache eher kreativ oder eher nervig? Warum? — Do you find youth language more creative or more annoying? Why?
  • Warum kann zu viel Umgangssprache in einer Bewerbung problematisch sein? — Why can too much colloquial language be a problem in a job application?
  • Sollte man als Lernender überhaupt Slang lernen, oder reicht die Standardsprache? — Should a learner bother to learn slang at all, or is the standard language enough?
