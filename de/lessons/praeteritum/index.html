German · A2 · Unit 6 — Talking About the Past · ~40 min

The Präteritum (war, hatte & common verbs)

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Understand that the Präteritum is the simple past — the narrative past of writing.
  • Use war (sein) and hatte (haben) in the Präteritum even when speaking.
  • Use the modal verbs in the Präteritum: konnte, musste, wollte, durfte, sollte, mochte.
  • Recognise weak (-te) versus strong (vowel-change) Präteritum forms.
  • Conjugate war and hatte across all persons.
  • Read and write a short narration in the Präteritum.

Dialogue

Oma

Als ich ein Kind war, hatten wir keinen Fernseher.

When I was a child, we didn't have a television.

Lena

Wirklich? Was habt ihr dann gemacht?

Really? What did you do then?

Oma

Wir mussten draußen spielen. Ich wollte immer Fußball spielen.

We had to play outside. I always wanted to play football.

Lena

Und durftest du das?

And were you allowed to?

Oma

Nicht immer. Ich sollte zuerst meine Hausaufgaben machen.

Not always. I was supposed to do my homework first.

Lena

Konntest du gut Fußball spielen?

Could you play football well?

Oma

Oh ja! Aber meine Schwester mochte den Sport nicht.

Oh yes! But my sister didn't like the sport.

Lena

Wo habt ihr damals gewohnt?

Where did you live back then?

Oma

Wir wohnten auf dem Land. Es war ruhig und schön.

We lived in the countryside. It was quiet and lovely.

Lena

Das klingt wie ein Märchen, Oma.

That sounds like a fairy tale, Grandma.

Grammar

Two past tenses, two jobs

German has two ordinary past tenses. The Perfekt (Lessons 16–17) rules everyday speech. The Präteritum (simple past, one word) is the narrative past of writing — novels, news reports, fairy tales (Es war einmal… = "Once upon a time…"). For most verbs you don't need the spoken Präteritum at A2; you just need to recognise it when reading.

But there is a big exception. A small group of very common verbs is normally used in the Präteritum even when speaking, because their Perfekt sounds clumsy: above all sein and haben, and the modal verbs.

war and hatte — learn these by heart

Nobody says Ich bin müde gewesen in casual talk; they say Ich war müde. Likewise Ich hatte Zeit, not Ich habe Zeit gehabt. Memorise both:

Notice that ich and er/sie/es are identical (war / hatte) — a hallmark of the Präteritum.

The modal verbs in the Präteritum

These too are normally said in the Präteritum. The umlaut disappears and a -te appears:

wollenwolltewanted to
dürfendurftewas allowed to

Endings work like hatte: ich konnte, du konntest, er konnte, wir konnten…

Weak vs strong forms (for reading)

When you meet the Präteritum in texts, two patterns appear. Weak (regular) verbs insert -te-: wohnen → wohnte, machen → machte, spielen → spielte. Strong (irregular) verbs change the stem vowel and add no -te: gehen → ging, kommen → kam, sehen → sah, geben → gab, fahren → fuhr. You mainly need to recognise these — the spoken Perfekt remains your active tool for them.

Pronunciation tip

Don't confuse war (was, German) with English war — the German vowel is a long, open ah: "vahr". And mochte (liked) versus möchte (would like): the Präteritum mochte has a plain o, while the polite present möchte has the rounded ö. One dot changes the tense and the meaning.

Reading: Eine kleine Geschichte

Es war einmal ein junger König. Er hatte ein großes Schloss, aber er war oft allein und traurig. Jeden Abend wollte er Geschichten hören, doch niemand konnte ihm welche erzählen.

Eines Tages kam eine alte Frau in das Schloss. Sie hatte viele Märchen im Kopf und sollte sie dem König erzählen. Der König war begeistert. Er musste lachen und weinen, und endlich war er nicht mehr allein.

Von damals an durfte die Frau immer im Schloss bleiben. So fand der König einen Freund.

Translation

Once upon a time there was a young king. He had a big castle, but he was often lonely and sad. Every evening he wanted to hear stories, yet no one could tell him any.

One day an old woman came to the castle. She had many fairy tales in her head and was supposed to tell them to the king. The king was thrilled. He had to laugh and cry, and at last he was no longer alone.

From then on the woman was allowed to stay in the castle for good. And so the king found a friend.

Culture note

Every German child grows up with the Präteritum through the Märchen of the Brothers Grimm — Hänsel und Gretel, Rotkäppchen (Little Red Riding Hood), Aschenputtel (Cinderella). They almost all open with the formula "Es war einmal…" and unfold entirely in the simple past, which is exactly why the Präteritum feels, to Germans, like the voice of storytelling. The Grimms collected these tales in the early 1800s in Kassel; the city today runs a Grimmwelt museum devoted to them.

There's a gentle north–south split, too. Speakers in the north use the spoken Präteritum a little more freely (you might hear "Ich ging…"), while the south and Austria lean almost entirely on the Perfekt. But everywhere, war, hatte and the modal forms like konnte and musste are the natural choice in speech — those you really should make active.

Talk about it

  • Wo hast du als Kind gewohnt? — Where did you live as a child?
  • Was wolltest du früher werden? — What did you want to become when you were younger?
  • Was musstest du als Kind zu Hause machen? — What did you have to do at home as a child?
  • Was konntest du mit zehn Jahren schon gut? — What could you already do well at the age of ten?
  • Wie war das Wetter gestern bei dir? — How was the weather where you are yesterday?
