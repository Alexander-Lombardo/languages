Grammar

Participles as adjectives: Partizip I vs. Partizip II

German has two participles, and either can sit in front of a noun and behave like an ordinary adjective — taking the same endings. The crucial thing is what each one means.

  • Partizip I = infinitive + -d (lesen → lesend, schlafen → schlafend). It is active and ongoing — the action is happening at the same time: der lesende Mann = the man who is reading.
  • Partizip II (the form you know from the Perfekt: gebaut, geschrieben, gekauft). With transitive verbs it is passive and completed: das gebaute Haus = the house that was built / has been built.
VerbPartizip I (active, ongoing)Partizip II (passive, done)
lesender lesende Student (who is reading)das gelesene Buch (that was read)
bauendie bauenden Arbeiter (who are building)das gebaute Haus (that was built)
weinendas weinende Kind (who is crying)—
kochendie kochende Suppe (boiling)die gekochte Suppe (cooked)

Both still take normal adjective endings according to gender, case and the preceding article: ein lesender Mann, mit dem gebauten Haus, die gekochten Eier.

The extended attribute (das erweiterte Attribut)

This is the hallmark structure. Around the participle you may build a whole phrase — objects, adverbs, prepositional phrases — and the entire block is squeezed between the article and the noun. Read it from the outside in: see the article, expect the noun, then unpack everything in front of it.

→ das vor zwei Jahren von der Stadt gebaute Museum = the museum (that was) built two years ago by the city. Notice that the participle keeps its adjective ending (-e) and sits right before the noun; everything else lines up to its left.

Transforming: relative clause ↔ participial attribute

Every extended attribute can be rewritten as a relative clause and vice versa. This is the single best way to understand the structure. To turn a relative clause into an attribute: drop the relative pronoun, move the participle to just before the noun, take everything between, and give the participle an adjective ending.

Relative clauseParticipial attribute
der Mann, der ein Buch liestder ein Buch lesende Mann
das Haus, das 1990 gebaut wurdedas 1990 gebaute Haus
die Frau, die gerade ankommtdie gerade ankommende Frau
die Reformen, die von der Regierung beschlossen wurdendie von der Regierung beschlossenen Reformen

The reverse rule: present-tense / active relative clause → Partizip I; passive (werden + Partizip II) relative clause → Partizip II.

The Gerundivum: future passive with zu

A special third option uses zu + Partizip I as an attribute. It carries a sense of must / can / is to be done — a future, modal passive.

  • das zu lösende Problem = the problem that is to be / must be solved (= das Problem, das gelöst werden muss).
  • die zu beachtenden Regeln = the rules to be observed.
  • ein nicht zu unterschätzender Faktor = a factor not to be underestimated.

This is dense, formal and very common in legal, technical and academic prose.

Pronunciation tip

When you read an extended attribute aloud, resist the urge to pause after the article. Keep das … gebaute Museum as one breath group, letting your voice rise slightly through the inserted block and then land on the noun. A pause after das signals that you've lost the thread; a smooth, unbroken arc signals that you've grasped the whole structure. The stressed syllable of the participle (ge-BAU-te) is your cue that the noun is about to arrive.

Reading: Eine Stilfrage

Der vor wenigen Jahren von der Stadt errichtete und inzwischen mehrfach ausgezeichnete Neubau gilt als Musterbeispiel nachhaltiger Architektur. Wer einen solchen Satz liest, erkennt sofort die Handschrift der gehobenen Schriftsprache: Zwischen Artikel und Nomen schiebt sich ein ganzes Bündel an Informationen, das in der gesprochenen Sprache gewöhnlich auf mehrere Relativsätze verteilt würde.

Diese erweiterten Partizipialattribute erlauben eine bemerkenswerte Verdichtung. Statt zu sagen „der Neubau, der vor wenigen Jahren von der Stadt errichtet wurde und der inzwischen mehrfach ausgezeichnet wurde“, packt der Verfasser den gesamten Sachverhalt vor das Nomen. Das spart Wörter und wirkt elegant – solange man es nicht übertreibt.

Denn genau darin liegt die Gefahr. Ein Attribut, das sich über vier Zeilen erstreckt, zwingt den Leser, das eigentliche Nomen lange im Gedächtnis zu behalten. Was als Zeichen sprachlicher Souveränität gedacht war, kippt dann ins Schwerfällige. Die Kunst besteht also nicht darin, möglichst viel voranzustellen, sondern darin, das richtige Maß zu finden.

Translation

The new building, erected by the city a few years ago and since then repeatedly awarded prizes, is considered a model example of sustainable architecture. Anyone who reads such a sentence immediately recognises the hallmark of elevated written language: between the article and the noun a whole bundle of information is inserted, which in spoken language would usually be spread across several relative clauses.

These extended participial attributes allow a remarkable compression. Instead of saying "the new building, which was erected by the city a few years ago and which has since been awarded prizes several times," the author packs the entire matter in front of the noun. It saves words and seems elegant — as long as one does not overdo it.

For that is precisely where the danger lies. An attribute that stretches over four lines forces the reader to hold the actual noun in memory for a long time. What was intended as a sign of linguistic mastery then tips over into the cumbersome. The art therefore lies not in preposing as much as possible, but in finding the right measure.

Culture note

The extended participial attribute is one of the clearest dividing lines between everyday German and the formal written register. You will rarely hear a four-line attribute over coffee, but you will meet it constantly in Qualitätszeitungen like the FAZ or the Süddeutsche Zeitung, in academic papers and above all in legal and bureaucratic German (Amtsdeutsch). German officialdom is famous — and often mocked — for sentences in which the reader must hold a noun in suspense while a thicket of modifiers unrolls before it.

Stylists from Ludwig Reiners to the modern Duden guides warn against overloading the Nominalklammer: good writers use the structure for genuine compression, then break it up before it strangles comprehension. Mastering the relative-clause ↔ attribute switch therefore isn't just grammar drill — it's a practical lever for adjusting the formality and density of your own prose.

Talk about it

  • Warum gelten erweiterte Attribute eher als Merkmal der Schrift- als der Sprechsprache? — Why are extended attributes considered a feature of written rather than spoken language?
  • Ab welchem Punkt kippt sprachliche Eleganz Ihrer Meinung nach in Schwerfälligkeit um? — At what point, in your view, does linguistic elegance tip over into clumsiness?
  • In welchen Textsorten – Recht, Wissenschaft, Journalismus – ist diese Verdichtung gerechtfertigt, in welchen nicht? — In which text types — law, science, journalism — is this compression justified, and in which is it not?
  • Verliert ein Text an Klarheit, wenn er Informationen vor das Nomen statt in Nebensätze packt? — Does a text lose clarity when it packs information in front of the noun rather than into subordinate clauses?
  • Sollte man Schülern raten, solche Strukturen zu vermeiden, oder sie zur Stilübung gezielt einsetzen? — Should learners be advised to avoid such structures, or to use them deliberately as a style exercise?
