Grammar

Subject pronouns: the words that do the action

A subject pronoun tells you who is doing something. German has six "slots", but a couple of words do double duty, so look carefully.

GermanEnglish
ichI
duyou (one person, informal)
er / sie / eshe / she / it
wirwe
ihryou (several people, informal)
sie / Siethey / you (formal)

Watch out for the three meanings of sie: lower-case sie can mean she (singular) or they (plural) — you tell them apart by the verb — and capital Sie means the polite you. Unlike English, ich is not capitalised unless it starts a sentence.

The verb sein (to be)

sein is the most important verb in German and, like "to be" in English, it is completely irregular — you simply learn the six forms.

PronounForm of seinExample
ichbinIch bin Markus.
dubistDu bist nett.
er/sie/esistSie ist aus Wien.
wirsindWir sind Studenten.
ihrseidIhr seid hier.
sie/SiesindSie sind aus der Schweiz.

Notice that wir, sie (they) and Sie (you formal) all take sind — three pronouns, one form.

Saying where you're from

Ask with Woher kommst du? (informal) or Woher kommen Sie? (formal). There are two natural ways to answer:

  • Ich komme aus + country/city: Ich komme aus Italien. Ich komme aus Berlin.
  • Ich bin aus + country/city: Ich bin aus Österreich.

Most countries are used without an article (aus Deutschland, aus Spanien), but a few take one and change form after aus: die Schweiz → aus der Schweiz, die USA → aus den USA. You'll meet the reason (the dative case) later; for now, learn these as set phrases.

Pronunciation tip

German r changes with its position. At the start of a syllable it's a soft throat-r (rot, kommen has the hard k but listen to Berlin), but at the end of a word the -er almost vanishes into a soft "-uh": Lehrer ≈ "LAY-uh", er ≈ "air". Also, du and er both have short, pure vowels — don't glide them like English "do" or "air".

Culture note

German is spoken as an official language in several countries, not just Germany. The three you'll hear about most are the DACH countries — Deutschland, Austria (Österreich) and the CH (Switzerland, from the Latin Confoederatio Helvetica). German is also official in Liechtenstein and parts of Belgium, Luxembourg and northern Italy (South Tyrol).

When Germans ask Woher kommst du? they may mean your country, but among Germans it often means which town or region — local origin matters, and accents from Hamburg, Cologne, Munich or Vienna are immediately recognisable. Answering with a city (Ich komme aus München) is perfectly normal and often invites a friendly follow-up.
