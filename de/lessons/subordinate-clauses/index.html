Subordinate Clauses (weil, dass, wenn)

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Use subordinating conjunctions (weil, dass, wenn, ob, obwohl, als, damit, bevor, nachdem) and send the conjugated verb to the end of the clause.
  • Place a comma before the subordinating conjunction.
  • Invert the main clause when the subordinate clause comes first (verb-comma-verb).
  • Distinguish subordinating from coordinating conjunctions (und, aber, oder, denn, sondern), which keep normal word order.
  • Express reasons, conditions, contrasts and reported facts in connected sentences.

Dialogue

Lena

Du, ich glaube, dass es heute noch regnet.

Hey, I think (that) it's going to rain today.

Tom

Wirklich? Ich bleibe lieber zu Hause, weil ich müde bin.

Really? I'd rather stay home, because I'm tired.

Lena

Obwohl du morgen eine Prüfung hast?

Although you have an exam tomorrow?

Tom

Genau deshalb. Ich lerne, damit ich sie bestehe.

Exactly because of that. I'm studying so that I pass it.

Lena

Gute Idee. Weißt du, ob die Bibliothek heute offen ist?

Good idea. Do you know whether the library is open today?

Tom

Ich denke schon. Bevor ich anfange, trinke ich noch einen Kaffee.

I think so. Before I start, I'll have a coffee first.

Lena

If you want, I'll come along and we'll study together.

Tom

Super. Nachdem wir gelernt haben, können wir einen Film schauen.

Great. After we've studied, we can watch a film.

Lena

Abgemacht. Ich hoffe nur, dass das Wetter besser wird.

Deal. I just hope (that) the weather gets better.

Tom

Und wenn nicht, bleiben wir eben drinnen.

And if not, we'll just stay inside.

Grammar

What a subordinate clause does

A Nebensatz (subordinate clause) cannot stand alone — it depends on a main clause. It begins with a subordinating conjunction, and that conjunction throws the conjugated verb all the way to the end of the clause. A comma always separates the two clauses. The most common subordinating conjunctions are:

ConjunctionMeaningConjunctionMeaning
weilbecauseobwohlalthough
dassthatalswhen (single past event)
wennif / when (repeated)damitso that
obwhetherbevorbefore
  nachdemafter

The verb goes to the end

Compare a normal main clause with the same idea inside a subordinate clause. The conjugated verb moves from position 2 to the very end:

Main clauseSubordinate clause
Es regnet.…, weil es regnet.
Ich bin müde.…, weil ich müde bin.
Sie hat keine Zeit.…, dass sie keine Zeit hat.
Er kommt mit.…, ob er mitkommt.

With a modal or perfect tense, the finite verb still ends the clause and the other verb sits just before it: …, weil ich lernen muss; …, weil ich gelernt habe. A separable verb rejoins as one word: …, weil er mitkommt.

When the subordinate clause comes first

You can put the subordinate clause first. Then the whole clause fills position 1 of the sentence, so the main clause must invert — its verb comes immediately after the comma. The pattern is verb-comma-verb (the two verbs almost touch across the comma):

  • Ich bleibe zu Hause, weil es regnet.
  • Weil es regnet, bleibe ich zu Hause.
  • Wenn du willst, komme ich mit.

Subordinating vs coordinating conjunctions

Be careful not to confuse the two families. Coordinating conjunctions (und, aber, oder, denn, sondern) simply join two equal clauses and do not change the word order — the verb stays in position 2:

TypeConjunctionsWord order
Coordinatingund, aber, oder, denn, sondernverb stays in position 2 (normal)
Subordinatingweil, dass, wenn, ob, obwohl, als…verb goes to the end

Compare: Ich bleibe zu Hause, denn es regnet. (coordinating — verb stays) vs Ich bleibe zu Hause, weil es regnet. (subordinating — verb at the end). denn and weil mean almost the same thing but behave completely differently.

Pronunciation tip

In speech, the comma before a subordinating conjunction is a real spoken pause — let your voice dip slightly before weil, dass or obwohl. And don't be alarmed by the verb pile-up at the end of clauses like …, weil ich gestern nicht kommen konnte: native speakers keep that final verb crisp and stressed, because it carries the key information. Train yourself to hold the verb until the end rather than blurting it early as in English.

Reading: Warum Jonas Deutsch lernt

Jonas lernt seit zwei Jahren Deutsch, weil er später in Deutschland studieren möchte. Er weiß, dass die Sprache nicht leicht ist, aber er gibt nicht auf. Obwohl er manchmal Fehler macht, spricht er jeden Tag ein bisschen mehr.

Wenn er ein neues Wort lernt, schreibt er es sofort in ein Heft. Bevor er schlafen geht, wiederholt er die Wörter noch einmal, damit er sie nicht vergisst. Nachdem er einen Film auf Deutsch gesehen hat, notiert er die Sätze, die ihm gefallen.

Seine Lehrerin sagt, dass Jonas große Fortschritte macht. Er hofft, dass er nächstes Jahr die Prüfung besteht, denn dann kann sein neues Leben in Berlin beginnen.

Translation

Jonas has been learning German for two years, because he wants to study in Germany later. He knows (that) the language isn't easy, but he doesn't give up. Although he sometimes makes mistakes, he speaks a little more every day.

When he learns a new word, he writes it down in a notebook right away. Before he goes to sleep, he reviews the words once more, so that he doesn't forget them. After he has watched a film in German, he notes down the sentences he likes.

His teacher says (that) Jonas is making great progress. He hopes (that) he'll pass the exam next year, because then his new life in Berlin can begin.

Culture note

The verb-at-the-end rule is the feature of German that most fascinates outsiders — Mark Twain joked in The Awful German Language that you have to wait for the verb at the end of a long sentence to find out what anyone actually did. In careful written German, especially academic and bureaucratic prose, those subordinate clauses can stack impressively, and holding the structure in your head until the final verb lands is a genuine skill native speakers take for granted.

In everyday speech, though, Germans often soften the strict rule: you'll frequently hear weil followed by main-clause word order — „Ich bleibe zu Hause, weil ich bin müde.“ — particularly among younger speakers. Teachers still mark it wrong, so learn the standard form, but don't be confused when you hear the colloquial version on the street.

Talk about it

  • Warum lernen Sie Deutsch? Antworten Sie mit einem Satz, der „weil“ benutzt. — Why are you learning German? Answer with a sentence using 'weil'.
  • Was machen Sie, wenn das Wetter schlecht ist? — What do you do when the weather is bad?
  • Glauben Sie, dass man eine Sprache am besten im Ausland lernt? Warum oder warum nicht? — Do you think (that) you learn a language best abroad? Why or why not?
  • Was machen Sie normalerweise, bevor Sie schlafen gehen? — What do you usually do before you go to sleep?
  • Obwohl Deutsch als schwer gilt, lernen es viele Menschen. Was motiviert Sie? — Although German is considered hard, many people learn it. What motivates you?
