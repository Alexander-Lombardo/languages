Telling Time, Days & Dates

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Ask and tell the time both the official way (13:30 Uhr) and the colloquial way (halb zwei, Viertel nach…).
  • Name the seven days of the week and the twelve months.
  • Use the time prepositions am (days), um (clock times) and im (months) correctly.
  • Say and write a date, including ordinal numbers (der erste Mai).
  • Ask Wie spät ist es? and Um wie viel Uhr…? and answer naturally.

Dialogue

Lena

Entschuldigung, wie spät ist es?

Excuse me, what time is it?

Tom

Es ist Viertel nach drei.

It's a quarter past three.

Lena

Schon so spät? Der Film beginnt um halb vier.

That late already? The film starts at half past three.

Tom

Kein Problem. Wir haben noch fünfzehn Minuten.

No problem. We still have fifteen minutes.

Lena

Gut. Und wann hast du am Montag Zeit?

Good. And when do you have time on Monday?

Tom

Am Montag arbeite ich bis sechs Uhr abends.

On Monday I work until six in the evening.

Lena

Und am Wochenende? Am Samstag?

And on the weekend? On Saturday?

Tom

Am Samstag bin ich frei. Treffen wir uns um zwei?

On Saturday I'm free. Shall we meet at two?

Lena

Perfekt. Mein Geburtstag ist auch bald — am ersten Mai.

Perfect. My birthday is soon too — on the first of May.

Tom

Im Mai? Dann feiern wir zusammen!

In May? Then we'll celebrate together!

Lena

Sehr gern. Also bis Samstag um zwei!

Gladly. So, see you Saturday at two!

Grammar

Telling the time: official vs. colloquial

To ask the time, say Wie spät ist es? or Wie viel Uhr ist es? The answer always starts with Es ist… German has two systems. The official system (timetables, TV, appointments) uses the 24-hour clock and the word Uhr: 13:30 → es ist dreizehn Uhr dreißig. The colloquial system uses the 12-hour clock with Viertel nach (quarter past), Viertel vor (quarter to) and halb.

Watch out for halb! German counts toward the next hour, so halb drei literally means "half on the way to three" = 2:30, not 3:30. This trips up every learner at first.

For "past" and "to" minutes you can also say zehn nach acht (8:10) and zwanzig vor neun (8:40).

Days, months and the prepositions am / um / im

The days are all masculine (der): Montag, Dienstag, Mittwoch, Donnerstag, Freitag, Samstag (or Sonnabend in the north), Sonntag. The months are also masculine: Januar, Februar, März, April, Mai, Juni, Juli, August, September, Oktober, November, Dezember.

Three little prepositions do most of the work. Learn them as fixed pairs:

PrepositionUse it forExample
am (an + dem)days & parts of the dayam Montag, am Wochenende, am Abend
umclock timesum acht Uhr, um halb drei
im (in + dem)months & seasonsim Juli, im Sommer

Saying the date

Dates use ordinal numbers. For 1–19 add -te to the number, from 20 add -ste: erste, zweite, dritte (irregular), vierte, fünfte … zwanzigste. With der/den the ending becomes -(s)te: Heute ist der erste Mai. When you ask, use Der Wievielte ist heute? (What's the date today?). In writing, a dot marks the ordinal: 1. Mai = der erste Mai.

  • Heute ist der dritte Oktober. — Today is the third of October.
  • Mein Geburtstag ist am zwölften Juni. — My birthday is on the twelfth of June.
  • Wir fahren im August nach Berlin. — We're travelling to Berlin in August.

Pronunciation tip

The word Uhr sounds like English "oor" — the German r after a vowel almost vanishes into a soft -uh. And in days of the week, the -tag ending is said "tahk" (the final g hardens to a k sound at the end of a word): Montag ≈ "MON-tahk".

Culture note

Germans run on the clock. Punctuality is a genuine sign of respect: arriving five minutes early is normal, and being late without warning is considered rude — whether for a doctor's appointment or a dinner with friends. Official schedules (trains, TV, opening hours) almost always use the 24-hour clock, so der Zug fährt um 14:07 Uhr is perfectly everyday.

One quirk worth knowing: the German week starts on Monday, not Sunday, and calendars are printed that way. The weekend (das Wochenende) is Saturday and Sunday, and on Sundays most shops are closed by law (Ladenschluss), so Germans do their shopping on Saturday.
