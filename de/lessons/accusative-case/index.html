The Accusative Case & kein

What you'll learn

  • Recognise the accusative case as the direct object of a sentence.
  • Change the masculine article in the accusative (der→den, ein→einen) and know that die/das/plural stay the same.
  • Negate a noun with kein/keine/keinen in the right form.
  • Use the accusative personal pronouns (mich, dich, ihn, sie, es, uns, euch, sie/Sie).
  • Use common accusative verbs: haben, brauchen, kaufen, essen, suchen.

Dialogue

Tom

We have a table, but no chair.

Anna

Schade. Brauchst du den Computer noch?

Too bad. Do you still need the computer?

Tom

No, go ahead and take it. I don't need it today.

Anna

Danke! Und hast du eine Lampe für mich?

Thanks! And do you have a lamp for me?

Tom

Eine Lampe? Ja, ich habe eine. Hier.

A lamp? Yes, I have one. Here.

Anna

Super. Kaufst du noch eine Zeitung?

Great. Are you also buying a newspaper?

No, I'm not buying a newspaper, just an apple.

Yes, I'll eat it in a moment. I'm hungry!

Anna

Dann suchen wir zusammen den Stuhl, ja?

Then let's look for the chair together, okay?

Grammar

What the accusative is

The accusative is the case of the direct object — the person or thing that the action happens to. In Ich kaufe den Apfel ("I buy the apple"), ich is the subject (nominative) and den Apfel is the direct object (accusative). German marks this by changing the article, not the noun ending.

The good news: only the masculine article changes. Feminine, neuter and plural articles look exactly the same as in the nominative. So you really only have to remember one thing: der → den, ein → einen, kein → keinen.

masculinefeminineneuterplural
Nominative (subject)der / ein / keindie / eine / keinedas / ein / keindie / — / keine
Accusative (object)den / einen / keinendie / eine / keinedas / ein / keindie / — / keine

Examples — notice the masculine change and the others staying put:

  • Ich habe einen Computer. (m) — I have a computer.
  • Ich habe eine Lampe. (f) — I have a lamp.
  • Ich habe ein Auto. (n) — I have a car.
  • Ich kaufe den Tisch. (m) — I'm buying the table.

The negation kein

kein means "no / not a / not any". Use it to negate a noun that has ein or no article at all. It takes the same endings as ein, plus a plural form. In the accusative the masculine becomes keinen.

QuestionNegative answer (accusative)
Hast du einen Stuhl? (m)Nein, ich habe keinen Stuhl.
Hast du eine Tasche? (f)Nein, ich habe keine Tasche.
Hast du ein Auto? (n)Nein, ich habe kein Auto.
Hast du Bücher? (pl)Nein, ich habe keine Bücher.

Remember: kein negates nouns; nicht negates verbs, adjectives and the rest of the sentence.

Accusative personal pronouns

Brauchst du den Computer? — Ja, ich brauche ihn. (the computer is masculine, so "it" = ihn). Common verbs that take an accusative object: haben, brauchen, kaufen, essen, suchen (and many more).

Pronunciation tip

Don't confuse kein ("kine", rhymes with English "fine") with klein (small). And the accusative -en ending on den / einen / keinen is unstressed — say it as a quick, swallowed -uhn, never a strong "EN".

Culture note

The accusative is your first real taste of the German case system — the feature that lets German shuffle word order around far more freely than English. Because den Apfel is visibly the object, a German can say Den Apfel esse ich ("The apple, I'm eating") and still be perfectly clear about who eats what. English, with no case endings, depends on word order to do the same job.

Take heart: A1 only asks you to master the masculine change (der → den) and kein. Get those automatic now and the dative and genitive cases later will feel far less daunting. A good habit from day one is to learn every noun together with its article and a sample accusative sentence.
