Dialogue

Anna

Hast du Geschwister, Tom?

Do you have siblings, Tom?

Tom

Ja, ich habe einen Bruder und eine Schwester.

Yes, I have a brother and a sister.

Anna

Wie heißt dein Bruder?

What is your brother's name?

Tom

Mein Bruder heißt Max. Meine Schwester heißt Lena.

My brother is called Max. My sister is called Lena.

Anna

Und habt ihr ein Haustier?

And do you have a pet?

Tom

Ja, wir haben einen Hund. Unser Hund heißt Bello.

Yes, we have a dog. Our dog is called Bello.

Anna

Wie groß ist deine Familie?

How big is your family?

Tom

Wir sind fünf: meine Eltern, meine Geschwister und ich.

We are five: my parents, my siblings and me.

Anna

Schön! Ich habe keine Geschwister, aber ich habe eine Katze.

Nice! I have no siblings, but I have a cat.

Tom

Wie heißt deine Katze?

What is your cat's name?

Anna

Sie heißt Mimi. Sie ist sehr klein.

She is called Mimi. She is very small.

Grammar

The verb haben (to have)

Haben is one of the most important verbs in German — you need it constantly, and later you'll use it to build the past tense. It is irregular, so learn it by heart. Notice the missing b in du hast and er/sie/es hat.

PronounFormExample
ichhabeIch habe Zeit.
duhastDu hast einen Hund.
er / sie / eshatSie hat eine Schwester.
wirhabenWir haben ein Auto.
ihrhabtHabt ihr Kinder?
sie / SiehabenSie haben eine Katze.

Possessive articles ("my, your, his, her…")

Possessive articles say who something belongs to. The stem changes with the owner, and the ending changes to agree with the gender of the thing owned. They follow the same pattern as ein/eine.

OwnerPossessive
ich → mymein
du → your (informal)dein
er → hissein
sie → herihr
es → itssein
wir → ourunser
ihr → your (plural)euer
sie → their / Sie → your (formal)ihr / Ihr

Agreeing with gender (nominative)

In the nominative, masculine and neuter take the bare stem, while feminine (and plural) add -e — exactly like ein/eine.

Gender of nounFormExample
masculinemeinmein Bruder
femininemeinemeine Schwester
neutermeinmein Kind
pluralmeinemeine Eltern

So it is her father = ihr Vater but her mother = ihre Mutter — the ending follows the thing owned, not the owner. As a preview, after ich habe a masculine noun takes einen: Ich habe einen Bruder, eine Schwester, ein Kind. You'll learn why in the accusative lesson.

Pronunciation tip

Many family words end in an unstressed -er — Vater, Mutter, Bruder, Schwester, Tochter — and in German this sounds like a soft -uh, not the hard American "-er": Mutter ≈ "MOOT-uh". Keep the stress firmly on the first syllable.

Culture note

German makes a clear distinction between Geschwister (siblings as a group) and the individual Bruder and Schwester, and there is no everyday single word for "sibling" in the singular — you simply say mein Bruder or meine Schwester. The words Mann and Frau do double duty: depending on context they mean "man/woman" or "husband/wife", so meine Frau usually means "my wife".

Family forms in German-speaking countries have diversified, and the language has kept pace: a Patchworkfamilie (blended family) is a common term, and many people speak of their Partner or Partnerin rather than husband or wife. When addressing relatives, children typically use the informal du with everyone in the family, including grandparents — formality is reserved for the world outside the home.
