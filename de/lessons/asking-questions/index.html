Asking Questions (W-Fragen & yes/no)

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Use the main W-question words: wer, was, wo, woher, wohin, wann, warum, wie, wie viel/viele, welche-.
  • Build a W-question with the question word first and the verb in second position.
  • Form a yes/no question by putting the conjugated verb at the very front.
  • Answer with ja, nein and — crucially — doch.
  • Tell the difference between wo, woher and wohin.

Dialogue

Frau Weber

Guten Tag! Wie heißen Sie?

Hello! What's your name?

Markus

Ich heiße Markus. Und Sie?

My name is Markus. And you?

Frau Weber

Ich bin Frau Weber. Woher kommen Sie?

I'm Mrs. Weber. Where are you from?

Markus

Ich komme aus Österreich. Wohin gehen Sie jetzt?

I'm from Austria. Where are you going now?

Frau Weber

Ich gehe ins Büro. Wann beginnt Ihr Kurs?

I'm going to the office. When does your course start?

Markus

Um neun Uhr. Warum fragen Sie?

At nine o'clock. Why do you ask?

Frau Weber

Wir haben denselben Kurs! Trinken Sie Kaffee?

We have the same course! Do you drink coffee?

Markus

Ja, gern. Haben wir noch Zeit?

Yes, gladly. Do we still have time?

Frau Weber

Sie mögen keinen Tee, oder?

You don't like tea, do you?

Markus

Doch, ich mag Tee! Aber jetzt Kaffee, bitte.

Yes I do, I like tea! But coffee now, please.

Frau Weber

Natürlich. Welches Café mögen Sie?

Of course. Which café do you like?

Grammar

W-questions (W-Fragen)

Most German question words start with W — handy, like English "wh-". In a W-question the question word comes first, and the conjugated verb stays in second position (the V2 rule you already know), with the subject right after it.

wo / woher / wohin are three different words: wo asks where something is, woher where it comes from, and wohin where it's going to. welche- changes its ending with gender: welcher (m), welche (f), welches (n), welche (pl).

Yes/no questions (Ja/Nein-Fragen)

To make a yes/no question, simply move the conjugated verb to the very front, before the subject. There is no extra word like English "do".

StatementQuestion
Du wohnst in Berlin.Wohnst du in Berlin?
Sie trinkt Kaffee.Trinkt sie Kaffee?
Ihr habt Zeit.Habt ihr Zeit?

Answering: ja, nein and doch

A normal yes/no question is answered with ja (yes) or nein (no). But there's a third answer that English lacks. When the question is negative and you want to contradict it ("yes, actually!"), you must use doch, not ja.

  • Magst du Tee? → Ja, ich mag Tee. / Nein, ich mag keinen Tee.
  • Magst du keinen Tee? (You don't like tea?) → Doch, ich mag Tee! (Yes I do!) / Nein, ich mag keinen Tee. (No, I don't.)

Pronunciation tip

Every W-word here begins with the German w, which sounds like English v: wo = "voh", was = "vahs", wie = "vee". And questions in German rise in pitch at the end just like in English, so let your voice go up on Trinkst du Kaffee?

Culture note

The little word doch is one of the most German things about German. As an answer it means "yes, on the contrary" and lets you flatly contradict a negative question or statement in a single syllable — something English needs a whole clause for ("Yes, I actually do!"). You'll also hear it sprinkled into sentences as a softening particle: Komm doch mit! ("Oh, do come along!"). For now, just master it as the answer to negative questions.

A polite tip: when you don't know someone, German prefers Sie-questions (Woher kommen Sie?) over the casual du. And a small-talk classic is Woher kommen Sie? — Germans really do ask, and they expect a region or country in return.
