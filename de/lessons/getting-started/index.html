Grammar

The German alphabet

German uses the same 26 letters as English, plus three umlauted vowels — ä, ö, ü — and one extra letter, ß (called Eszett or scharfes S), which sounds like a sharp ss. The umlaut (the two dots) genuinely changes the sound, so schon (already) and schön (beautiful) are different words.

The consonants that trip people up

A few letters are not pronounced the English way:

  • w = English v: was, Wein, wir.
  • v = English f: Vater, vier, von.
  • z = ts: Zeit, zehn, Zug.
  • j = English y: ja, Jahr, jung.
  • s before a vowel = z: sehen, Sonne; ß/ss = a sharp s.
  • ch = a soft, breathy sound after e/i (ich, nicht) and a harder throat sound after a/o/u (Bach, acht).
  • sp- and st- at the start of a word = shp and sht: Sprache, Straße.

Pronunciation tip

The single most useful spelling rule in German: every noun is capitalised, anywhere in the sentence — der Tag, das Brot, die Frau. It looks strange at first but it actually helps you spot the nouns. Also, the German r is made at the back of the throat (a light gargle), and a final -er sounds almost like a soft -uh: Vater ≈ "FAH-tuh".

Culture note

Germans tend to keep Sie (the formal "you") with strangers, shopkeepers and colleagues until they're invited to switch to the informal du — so when in doubt, use Sie. Greetings shift with the clock: Guten Morgen in the morning, Guten Tag through the day, Guten Abend in the evening. In the south and in Austria you'll hear Grüß Gott instead of Guten Tag, and in Switzerland a friendly Grüezi.
