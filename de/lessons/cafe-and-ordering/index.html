At the Café: Ordering Food & Drink

What you'll learn

  • Order politely with the fixed forms Ich möchte… and Ich hätte gern…
  • Use the accusative case to name what you want (einen Kaffee, ein Wasser, eine Suppe).
  • Understand and use café phrases: Was darf es sein?, Zusammen oder getrennt?, Die Rechnung, bitte.
  • Name common foods and drinks and ask the price with Was kostet das?
  • Handle a short café exchange from greeting to paying.

Dialogue

Kellner

Guten Tag! Was darf es sein?

Good day! What can it be? (= What would you like?)

Lena

Guten Tag. Ich hätte gern einen Kaffee, bitte.

Good day. I'd like a coffee, please.

Kellner

Gern. Und möchten Sie auch etwas essen?

Gladly. And would you like something to eat as well?

Lena

Ja, ich möchte ein Stück Kuchen.

Yes, I'd like a piece of cake.

Kellner

Sehr gern. Und für Sie, der Herr?

With pleasure. And for you, sir?

Tom

Ich nehme eine Suppe und ein Wasser, bitte.

I'll take a soup and a water, please.

Kellner

Kommt sofort.

Coming right up.

Lena

Der Kuchen ist wirklich lecker!

The cake is really delicious!

Tom

Entschuldigung, die Rechnung, bitte.

Excuse me, the bill, please.

Kellner

Natürlich. Zusammen oder getrennt?

Of course. Together or separately?

Tom

Zusammen, bitte.

Together, please.

Kellner

Das macht zwölf Euro fünfzig.

That comes to twelve euros fifty.

Grammar

Ordering politely: Ich möchte… and Ich hätte gern…

To order in a café you almost never say Ich will ("I want") — it sounds blunt. Instead Germans use two polite, ready-made forms. Learn them now as fixed phrases; you'll meet the full grammar behind them in the modal-verb lesson.

FormMeaningExample
Ich möchte…I would like…Ich möchte einen Tee.
Ich hätte gern…I'd like… (very polite)Ich hätte gern eine Suppe.
Ich nehme…I'll take/have… (casual)Ich nehme einen Kaffee.

All three are followed by the thing you want in the accusative case — that's the case for the object you're ordering.

The accusative: what you order

The word for "a/an" changes with gender, and the masculine form changes in the accusative from ein to einen. Feminine and neuter stay the same as the dictionary form.

GenderSubject (the…)Accusative (a…)
masculineder KaffeeIch möchte einen Kaffee.
femininedie SuppeIch möchte eine Suppe.
neuterdas WasserIch möchte ein Wasser.

So gender matters every time you order: einen Saft (der Saft), eine Cola (die Cola), ein Bier (das Bier).

Café phrases you will hear

  • Was darf es sein? — What would you like? (the waiter's opener)
  • Möchten Sie etwas trinken / essen? — Would you like something to drink / eat?
  • Zusammen oder getrennt? — Together or separately? (one bill or split)
  • Die Rechnung, bitte. — The bill, please.
  • Das macht … Euro. — That comes to … euros.
  • Stimmt so. — Keep the change.

Pronunciation tip

The ö in möchte is the same rounded sound as in schön: shape your lips for "oo" but try to say "ay". And note the soft ch in möchte and Brötchen — it's a gentle, breathy sound made near the front of the mouth, not a hard "k".

Culture note

In a German café you usually seat yourself, and the waiter comes to you — there's rarely a counter queue for table service. Tipping (das Trinkgeld) is modest: round up or add about 5–10%, and crucially you say the total you want to pay as you hand over the money ("Zwölf fünfzig? Machen Sie dreizehn.") rather than leaving coins on the table. When the waiter asks Zusammen oder getrennt?, splitting the bill is completely normal and no one bats an eye.

Coffee culture runs deep, especially the afternoon ritual of Kaffee und Kuchen around 3–4 p.m. — a slice of cake (Schwarzwälder Kirschtorte, Apfelkuchen) with coffee, often on weekends with family. Tap water is not served automatically; if you order Wasser you'll usually get bottled, either mit (sparkling) or ohne Kohlensäure (still).
