Nouns, Gender & Articles (der/die/das)

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Understand that every German noun has a grammatical gender — masculine, feminine or neuter.
  • Use the definite articles der, die, das and the indefinite ein, eine in the nominative.
  • Recognise the main plural patterns (-e, -(e)n, -er, -s) and umlaut changes.
  • Remember that every German noun is always capitalised.
  • Learn new nouns together with their article, not on their own.

Dialogue

Lena

Was ist das?

What is that?

Tom

Das ist ein Tisch. Der Tisch ist neu.

That is a table. The table is new.

Lena

Und das? Ist das eine Lampe?

And that? Is that a lamp?

Tom

Ja, das ist eine Lampe. Die Lampe ist schön.

Yes, that is a lamp. The lamp is beautiful.

Lena

Ist das ein Buch oder ein Heft?

Is that a book or a notebook?

Tom

Das ist ein Buch. Das Buch ist interessant.

That is a book. The book is interesting.

Lena

Und wer ist das? Ein Mann?

And who is that? A man?

Tom

Nein, das ist eine Frau. Die Frau heißt Anna.

No, that is a woman. The woman is called Anna.

Lena

Ah, and there are two chairs.

Tom

Genau. Die Stühle sind auch neu.

Exactly. The chairs are also new.

Lena

Alles ist neu hier!

Everything is new here!

Grammar

Every noun has a gender

In German, every noun is masculine, feminine or neuter. This grammatical gender often has nothing to do with biology: der Tisch (table) is masculine, die Lampe (lamp) is feminine, and even das Mädchen (girl) is neuter. Because the gender is unpredictable, you must learn every noun together with its article — say der Tisch, never just Tisch.

GenderDefinite ("the")Example
masculinederder Mann, der Hund, der Apfel
femininediedie Frau, die Lampe, die Tür
neuterdasdas Haus, das Buch, das Kind

Definite vs indefinite articles (nominative)

The definite article (der/die/das = "the") points to something specific. The indefinite article (ein/eine = "a/an") introduces something for the first time. In the nominative case (the subject form), masculine and neuter both take ein, while feminine takes eine.

Genderthea / an
masculineder Tischein Tisch
femininedie Lampeeine Lampe
neuterdas Buchein Buch

So: Das ist ein Tisch. Der Tisch ist neu. First you introduce it (ein), then you refer back to it (der).

Making plurals

German plurals are not as simple as adding -s. There are several patterns, and you learn them with the word. In the plural the article is always die, whatever the original gender.

PatternSingularPlural
add -e (often + umlaut)der Tischdie Tische
add -e + umlautder Stuhldie Stühle
add -(e)ndie Lampedie Lampen
add -er (+ umlaut)das Buchdie Bücher
add -s (foreign words)das Autodie Autos
no change (umlaut only)der Apfeldie Äpfel

Capital letters

Remember the golden rule from Lesson 0: every noun is capitalised, wherever it appears in a sentence — Der Hund spielt mit der Katze. If you can put der, die or das in front of a word, capitalise it.

Pronunciation tip

Listen for the article, not just the noun, when you learn vocabulary — the rhythm der-Tísch, die-Lámpe, das-Búch helps the gender stick. Note the umlaut in plurals genuinely changes the vowel: Buch has the long oo of "boot", but Bücher uses the ü sound — say ee with rounded lips.

Culture note

The gender of German nouns is one of the first real hurdles for learners, and even native speakers occasionally disagree on rare or new words. Dictionaries always list a noun's gender, usually as der/die/das or with the symbols m, f, n. There are some helpful tendencies — words ending in -ung, -heit, -keit and -tion are almost always feminine, while days, months and seasons (der Montag, der Mai, der Sommer) are masculine — but the safest habit is simply to memorise the article with every new word.

The capitalisation of all nouns is unique to German among major European languages. It was standardised in the 18th century and survived the spelling reforms of 1996–2006, when a proposal to abolish it was rejected. For learners it is actually a gift: at a glance you can tell which words in a sentence are the "things".
