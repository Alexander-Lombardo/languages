German Varieties (Germany, Austria, Switzerland)

What you'll learn

  • Understand that German is a pluricentric language with three equal standard varieties: Federal German, Austrian German and Swiss Standard German.
  • Recognise key lexical differences across DE, AT and CH (Jänner/Januar, Sackerl/Tüte, Paradeiser/Tomate, Semmel/Brötchen).
  • Know the Swiss spelling rule: no ß — always ss.
  • Identify regional variation within Germany (north vs south: Moin, Grüß Gott, Brötchen/Semmel/Schrippe).
  • Adapt greetings, register and politeness to the variety you are speaking.

Dialogue

Frau Weber (DE)

Guten Tag! Ich hätte gern zwei Brötchen, bitte.

Hello! I'd like two rolls, please.

Verkäufer (AT)

Servus! Bei uns heißen die Semmeln. Zwei Semmeln also, kommt sofort.

Hi! Here we call them Semmeln. Two rolls then, coming right up.

Frau Weber (DE)

Ach so. Und haben Sie auch Tomaten und Aprikosen?

I see. And do you also have tomatoes and apricots?

Grammar

Pluricentric German: one language, three standards

German is a plurizentrische Sprache. Alongside bundesdeutsches Hochdeutsch there are two further full standards: österreichisches Deutsch and Schweizer Hochdeutsch (Swiss Standard German, the written/formal form — distinct from spoken Schweizerdeutsch dialect). These are not "wrong" or "dialect": they are codified, taught and used in official documents. Austrian terms appear officially in the EU (e.g. Erdäpfel, Marillen are protected Austrian designations).

Key terms: an Austriazismus is a feature specific to Austrian Standard German; a Helvetismus is one specific to Swiss Standard German; a Teutonismus (less common term) is one specific to Germany.

Swiss spelling: no ß

The single most visible Helvetismus is orthographic: Swiss Standard German has abolished the ß entirely. Wherever Germany and Austria write ß, Switzerland writes ss:

  • Straße → Strasse
  • Fuß → Fuss
  • heißen → heissen
  • Maß → Mass (note: the long/short vowel distinction that ß vs ss usually signals is simply lost in writing).

Swiss German also tends to keep more French loanwords (das Velo, das Trottoir for pavement, das Billett for ticket, merci for thanks).

Variation within Germany: north vs. south

Germany itself is far from uniform. The clearest split runs north–south:

  • Greetings: Moin (north, used all day, not just morning) vs. Grüß Gott / Servus (Bavaria, the south).
  • The roll: Brötchen (most of Germany) vs. Semmel (Bavaria) vs. Schrippe (Berlin) vs. Wecken (south-west).
  • Saturday: Samstag (south/west) vs. Sonnabend (north/east).
  • Pronunciation: the south has a clearer rolled or harder r and pronounces the -ig ending as -ig/-ick, while the standard north says -ich (König = "Köni-ch").

Register and politeness shift too: Austrians and southerners reach for titles and the warm Servus more readily; the Swiss are famously reserved and formal in writing; northerners value terse directness — Moin can be a complete conversation.

Pronunciation tip

You can place a speaker by ear in seconds. Austrian and Bavarian speech softens consonants and sings on a gentle melody; the -ig ending is hard (fertig = "fertik"). Swiss Standard German has a notably guttural, scratchy ch (much further back than the northern ich-sound) and a distinctly melodic, sing-song intonation. Northern German is flatter and crisper, with a clean -ich for -ig. When you read the Swiss spellings aloud, remember the written ss doesn't tell you the vowel length — you simply have to know that Strasse still has a short a and Mass a long one.

Reading: Eine Sprache, drei Standards

Deutsch ist eine plurizentrische Sprache: Es gibt nicht eine einzige richtige Norm, sondern drei gleichberechtigte Standardvarietäten — die bundesdeutsche, die österreichische und die schweizerische. Wer in Wien »Jänner« sagt, spricht keinen Dialekt, sondern korrektes Standarddeutsch; nur in Deutschland heißt der Monat »Januar«.

Die Unterschiede sind oft genüsslich kulinarisch. In Deutschland kauft man Tomaten, Aprikosen und Kartoffeln; in Österreich Paradeiser, Marillen und Erdäpfel. Das Brötchen wird im Süden zur Semmel, in Berlin zur Schrippe. Und in der Schweiz fährt man nicht mit dem Fahrrad, sondern mit dem Velo, und telefoniert nicht mit dem Handy, sondern mit dem Natel.

Eine schweizerische Besonderheit fällt sofort ins Auge: Das ß gibt es dort nicht. Man schreibt konsequent »ss« — also »Strasse«, »Fuss«, »heissen«. Auch die Begrüßung verrät die Herkunft: »Moin« im Norden, »Grüß Gott« im Süden und in Österreich, »Grüezi« in der Schweiz. Wer all das kennt, hört einem Sprecher die Heimat schon nach wenigen Worten an.

Translation

German is a pluricentric language: there is not a single correct norm, but three equally valid standard varieties — the Federal German, the Austrian and the Swiss. Whoever says "Jänner" in Vienna is not speaking dialect but correct Standard German; only in Germany is the month called "Januar".

The differences are often enjoyably culinary. In Germany you buy Tomaten, Aprikosen and Kartoffeln; in Austria Paradeiser, Marillen and Erdäpfel. The bread roll becomes a Semmel in the south and a Schrippe in Berlin. And in Switzerland you don't ride a Fahrrad but a Velo, and you don't phone with a Handy but with a Natel.

One Swiss peculiarity catches the eye at once: there is no ß. They consistently write "ss" — so "Strasse", "Fuss", "heissen". The greeting too betrays one's origin: "Moin" in the north, "Grüß Gott" in the south and in Austria, "Grüezi" in Switzerland. Whoever knows all this can tell a speaker's home after just a few words.

Culture note

The three standards are politically real, not academic hair-splitting. Austria negotiated a protocol with the EU listing 23 specifically Austrian food terms — Erdäpfel, Marillen, Faschiertes, Topfen — that keep their validity in EU documents, a small but fierce point of national pride. In Switzerland, the absence of the ß is not laziness but settled orthographic policy dating to the early twentieth century; Swiss keyboards simply have no ß key.

Spoken reality adds another layer. In German-speaking Switzerland, everyday life is conducted in Schweizerdeutsch, a set of Alemannic dialects so distinct that Germans often cannot follow them; Schweizer Hochdeutsch is reserved for writing, news broadcasts and talking to foreigners. So a Swiss person reading the news and the same person chatting in a café can sound like two different languages — a striking case of Diglossie (diglossia) that is part of everyday life.

Talk about it

  • Warum spricht man von »plurizentrischer Sprache« statt von »Hochdeutsch und seinen Abweichungen«? — Why do we speak of a 'pluricentric language' rather than 'High German and its deviations'?
  • Sollte man als Deutschlernender eine Varietät bewusst wählen, oder ist das egal, solange man verstanden wird? — Should a learner deliberately choose one variety, or does it not matter as long as one is understood?
  • Was sagt die Abschaffung des ß in der Schweiz über das Verhältnis von Norm und Praxis aus? — What does the abolition of the ß in Switzerland tell us about the relationship between norm and practice?
  • Inwiefern ist der Gebrauch eines regionalen Wortes wie »Semmel« oder »Schrippe« auch eine Frage von Identität und Zugehörigkeit? — To what extent is using a regional word like 'Semmel' or 'Schrippe' also a question of identity and belonging?
  • Welche Vorurteile knüpfen sich an die einzelnen Varietäten — und woher kommen sie? — Which stereotypes attach to the individual varieties — and where do they come from?
