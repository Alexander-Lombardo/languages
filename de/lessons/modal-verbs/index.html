Modal Verbs (können, müssen, wollen…)

What you'll learn

  • Conjugate the six modal verbs können, müssen, wollen, dürfen, sollen and möchten in the present.
  • Notice that the ich and er/sie/es forms take no ending and are identical (ich kann, er kann).
  • Build the sentence bracket (Satzklammer): modal in 2nd position, infinitive at the end.
  • Express the core meaning of each modal: ability, necessity, wish, permission, advice, polite want.
  • Form questions and negatives with a modal verb.

Dialogue

Markus

Kannst du heute Abend kommen?

Can you come this evening?

Lena

Leider nein. Ich muss heute arbeiten.

Unfortunately no. I have to work today.

Markus

Schade. Willst du morgen ins Kino?

Too bad. Do you want to go to the cinema tomorrow?

Lena

Ja, gern! Aber ich kann nicht spät bleiben.

Yes, gladly! But I can't stay late.

Markus

Kein Problem. Wir müssen nicht lange bleiben.

No problem. We don't have to stay long.

Lena

Soll ich die Karten kaufen?

Should I buy the tickets?

Markus

Nein, das mache ich. Du sollst dich nur entspannen.

No, I'll do that. You should just relax.

Lena

Markus

Natürlich darfst du. Je mehr, desto besser.

Of course you may. The more the merrier.

Lena

Super. Dann möchte ich auch Tom fragen.

Great. Then I'd like to ask Tom too.

Grammar

What modal verbs do

Modal verbs change the meaning of another verb — they add ability, necessity, permission, a wish and so on. The other verb stays in the infinitive (its dictionary form). German has six core modals:

ModalCore meaningExample
könnencan / be able toIch kann schwimmen.
müssenmust / have toIch muss arbeiten.
wollenwant toIch will schlafen.
dürfenmay / be allowed toIch darf rauchen.
sollenshould / be supposed toIch soll helfen.
möchtenwould like to (polite)Ich möchte bezahlen.

The irregular present conjugation

Modals are irregular. Most of them change their vowel in the singular, and — this is the key point — the ich and er/sie/es forms take no ending and look identical: ich kann, er kann.

Notice the vowel shift in the singular: o → a (sollen keeps o), ü → u, o → i, ü → a. sollen is the only one with no vowel change.

The sentence bracket (Satzklammer)

This is the heart of modal-verb word order. The conjugated modal goes in second position, and the infinitive jumps to the very end of the sentence. Everything else sits in between, forming a "bracket".

Ichmussheutearbeiten.
Wirwollenmorgen ins Kinogehen.
Dukannstgut Deutschsprechen.

In yes/no questions the modal moves to the front, and the infinitive still stays at the end: Kannst du heute kommen? To negate, put nicht before the infinitive: Ich kann heute nicht kommen.

Pronunciation tip

The umlauts carry meaning, so keep them crisp: können uses the rounded ö, müssen and dürfen the rounded ü. Compare du musst (no umlaut, "oo") with the infinitive müssen (umlaut, "ue"). The double consonant after the vowel (in muss, kann, will) keeps that vowel short and clipped.

Culture note

The difference between müssen and dürfen matters in daily German life, where rules are often stated plainly. Signs use them directly: Hier dürfen Sie nicht parken (You may not park here) or Sie müssen einen Termin machen (You must make an appointment). Note one famous trap: nicht müssen means "don't have to" (no obligation), while "must not" is expressed with nicht dürfen — so Du musst nicht kommen means you needn't come, not that you're forbidden.

Germans also lean on modals to sound polite and indirect: Könnten Sie…? (Could you…?) and Ich möchte… soften requests, while sollen is the everyday way to ask for or report instructions (Soll ich helfen? — Shall I help?). Mastering the six modals unlocks a huge share of natural conversation.
