Shopping, Quantities & Prices

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Ask the price with Was kostet…? and Wie viel kostet…?
  • Give quantities and packaging: ein Kilo, ein halbes Pfund, eine Flasche, eine Packung, ein bisschen, etwas.
  • Choose correctly between viel (uncountable) and viele (countable).
  • Use es gibt + accusative to say what is available.
  • Say and understand prices in euros and cents at a market or shop.

Dialogue

Verkäufer

Guten Morgen! Was darf es sein?

Good morning! What would you like?

Anna

Guten Morgen. Ich hätte gern ein Kilo Äpfel.

Good morning. I'd like a kilo of apples.

Verkäufer

Gern. Sonst noch etwas?

Gladly. Anything else?

Anna

Ja, ein halbes Pfund Käse, bitte. Was kostet der Käse?

Yes, half a pound of cheese, please. How much is the cheese?

Verkäufer

Der Käse kostet vier Euro neunzig pro Pfund.

The cheese costs four euros ninety per pound.

Anna

Gut. Und gibt es heute frische Eier?

Good. And are there fresh eggs today?

Verkäufer

Ja, es gibt eine Packung mit zehn Eiern.

Yes, there's a pack of ten eggs.

Anna

Perfekt, die nehme ich. Wie viel kostet alles zusammen?

Perfect, I'll take it. How much is everything together?

Verkäufer

Das macht acht Euro fünfzig.

That comes to eight euros fifty.

Anna

Hier sind zehn Euro.

Here's ten euros.

Verkäufer

Danke, und eins fünfzig zurück. Schönen Tag!

Thanks, and one fifty back. Have a nice day!

Grammar

Asking the price

There are two everyday ways to ask how much something costs. Both are followed by the thing in the nominative (the subject).

QuestionMeaning
Was kostet der Käse?What does the cheese cost?
Wie viel kostet das Brot?How much does the bread cost?
Was kosten die Äpfel?What do the apples cost? (plural → kosten)

To answer, say … kostet … Euro, or for a total: Das macht … Euro. Prices are spoken as two numbers: 4,90 € = "vier Euro neunzig", and 1,50 € = "eins fünfzig".

Quantities and packaging

To buy an amount, just put the quantity word in front of the food — no "of" is needed in German.

viel vs viele, and es gibt

Use viel with things you can't count (mass nouns) and viele with things you can count (plurals).

  • viel + uncountable: viel Geld, viel Milch, viel Käse.
  • viele + countable plural: viele Äpfel, viele Eier, viele Leute.

To say what's available, use es gibt ("there is / there are") — and what follows is in the accusative, so masculine ein becomes einen:

  • Es gibt einen Markt am Samstag. — There's a market on Saturday.
  • Es gibt frische Eier. — There are fresh eggs.
  • Gibt es einen Supermarkt hier? — Is there a supermarket here?

Pronunciation tip

The price comma is spoken, not the word "point": 3,20 € is "drei Euro zwanzig" or simply "drei zwanzig". Watch the pf at the start of Pfund — both letters are pronounced, a quick "p" straight into "f". And ä in Käse is a long open "e", like the vowel in English air.

Culture note

Open-air weekly markets (der Wochenmarkt) are a fixture of German town life, usually on a central square once or twice a week. You'll see prices written with a comma (2,99 €) and often pro Kilo or pro Stück (per piece). A traditional Pfund is exactly 500 grams, so half a pound is 250 g — handy at the cheese or sausage counter where you order by weight.

At the supermarket checkout, pack your own bags and pack fast — German cashiers are famously quick. Plastic bags cost money, so most people bring a reusable Stofftasche or the classic Jutebeutel. Bottles carry a deposit (das Pfand): you pay a little extra and get it back at the Pfandautomat when you return the empties. And many smaller shops and markets still prefer cash, so it's wise to carry some coins.
