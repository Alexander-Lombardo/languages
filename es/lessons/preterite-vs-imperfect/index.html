Dialogue

Marta

¿Qué hiciste anoche?

What did you do last night?

Diego

Estudiaba para el examen cuando me llamó mi hermano.

I was studying for the exam when my brother called me.

Marta

¿Y qué pasó?

And what happened?

Diego

Me dijo que ganó la lotería. ¡De repente todo cambió!

He told me he won the lottery. Suddenly everything changed!

Marta

¡No puede ser! ¿Cómo estaba él?

No way! How was he?

Diego

Estaba muy feliz. Gritaba y reía sin parar.

He was very happy. He was shouting and laughing nonstop.

Marta

De niño, él siempre decía que iba a ser rico.

As a child, he always used to say he was going to be rich.

Diego

Es verdad. Anoche su sueño se hizo realidad.

It's true. Last night his dream came true.

Marta

¿Y tú? ¿Terminaste de estudiar?

And you? Did you finish studying?

Diego

No. Estaba tan emocionado que cerré los libros.

No. I was so excited that I closed the books.

Marta

¡Qué recuerdo tan increíble!

What an incredible memory!

Grammar

Two past tenses, two jobs

Spanish has two simple past tenses. The preterite tells you what happened — a single, finished action. The imperfect sets the scene — what was happening, what used to happen, or a description.

Use the PRETERITE for…Use the IMPERFECT for…
A completed action: llegué (I arrived)Background / ongoing: llovía (it was raining)
A series of finished eventsHabits: siempre jugaba (I always played)
A sudden interruption: de repenteAge, time, weather, feelings, descriptions

Only three verbs are irregular in the imperfect: ser (era), ir (iba), ver (veía).

They work together

In a story the imperfect paints the background and the preterite drops in the action: Yo dormía (imperfect) cuando sonó (preterite) el teléfono. — I was sleeping when the phone rang.

Pronunciation tip

The imperfect accent: all -er/-ir imperfect forms carry an accent on the í: comía, vivía, tenía. Push hard on that EE sound.

-aba endings have a soft Spanish b (almost a v): hablaba = ah-BLAH-bah. Keep the stress on the second-to-last syllable.

Culture note

The preterite/imperfect split feels strange to English speakers because English uses one simple past for both. In Latin-American storytelling — from abuela's bedtime tales to telenovelas — the imperfect creates that dreamy “había una vez” (once upon a time) background, while the preterite delivers the twists. Listen for how speakers slow into the imperfect to describe a scene, then snap to the preterite when something finally happens.
