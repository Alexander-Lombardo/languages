Marta

Grammar

Every noun has a gender

In Spanish, every noun is either masculine or feminine — even objects. This is not about real-life gender; it's just grammar. The good news: the ending usually tells you which one.

  • Nouns ending in -o are usually masculine: el libro, el niño.
  • Nouns ending in -a are usually feminine: la mesa, la casa.
  • Endings like -ción, -dad are feminine: la ciudad.
  • Watch out: el día (m.) and la mano (f.) break the rule. Learn each noun with its article.

The articles

Definite ("the") and indefinite ("a / some") articles must match the noun in gender and number.

a / someununaunosunas

So: el libro → los libros; una silla → unas sillas.

Pronunciation tip

The article -s never changes the vowel. Say los as "lohs" and las as "lahs" — short and clean, no English "uh".

Curious case: we say el agua (not la agua) because agua starts with a stressed "a" sound — but it's still feminine, so its plural is las aguas. This trick keeps two a-sounds from clashing.

Culture note

Grammatical gender feels strange to English speakers, but it's normal across most European languages. A practical habit pays off fast: always learn a noun together with its article — say la mesa, not just mesa. Spanish speakers do the same instinctively, and it saves you from guessing later. When the gender isn't obvious (like el día or la mano), the article is your anchor.
