Dialogue

Marta

¡Hola, Diego! ¿Cómo estás hoy?

Hi, Diego! How are you today?

Diego

Bien, pero tengo mucha hambre.

Good, but I'm very hungry.

Marta

¿Quieres comer algo ahora?

Do you want to eat something now?

Diego

Sí, pero no puedo. Tengo que trabajar.

Yes, but I can't. I have to work.

Marta

¿Vas al trabajo ahora?

Are you going to work now?

Diego

Sí, voy ahora. Tengo mucha prisa.

Yes, I'm going now. I'm in a big hurry.

Marta

¿Qué haces en el trabajo?

What do you do at work?

Diego

Hago café. Trabajo en un café.

I make coffee. I work in a coffee shop.

Marta

¡Qué bien! ¿Cuántos años tienes?

How nice! How old are you?

Diego

Tengo veinte años. ¿Y tú?

I'm twenty years old. And you?

Marta

Tengo veintidós. Bueno, ¡tienes razón, vete!

I'm twenty-two. Okay, you're right, go!

Diego

Gracias. ¡Vengo luego! Adiós.

Thanks. I'll come by later! Bye.

Grammar

Tener — "to have"

Tener is one of the most useful verbs in Spanish. It is irregular: the yo form ends in -go, and the e changes to ie in three forms.

PronounTenerEnglish
yotengoI have
tútienesyou have
él / ella / ustedtienehe/she has, you have
nosotrostenemoswe have
vosotrostenéisyou all have (Spain)
ellos / ellas / ustedestienenthey/you all have

Tener for age and feelings

Where English uses to be, Spanish often uses tener:

  • Tengo veinte años. = I am twenty (lit. I have twenty years).
  • Tengo hambre / sed. = I'm hungry / thirsty.
  • Tengo frío / calor. = I'm cold / hot.
  • Tengo prisa. = I'm in a hurry. Tienes razón. = You're right.
  • Tengo que trabajar. = I have to work (tener que + verb = have to).

Querer and poder are often followed by another verb in its plain form: Quiero comer. (I want to eat.) No puedo ir. (I can't go.)

Pronunciation tip

The g in tengo and hago: here the g is a hard 'g' like in go, because it comes before o. Compare with gente, where g before e is a raspy 'h'.

Diphthongs ie and ue: in tienes and puedo, glide the two vowels together quickly: TYEH-nehs, PWEH-doh. Don't add an extra syllable.

Culture note

Using tener for age and bodily states is one of the first big mindset shifts for English speakers. You don't are hungry in Spanish — you have hunger: tengo hambre. The same pattern covers thirst, cold, heat, fear and being right. Memorising these set phrases early makes you sound natural fast, because native speakers use them constantly in daily life across all 20 Spanish-speaking countries.
