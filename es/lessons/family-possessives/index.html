Family & Possessives

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Name the core members of your family in Spanish.
  • Use possessive adjectives (mi, tu, su, nuestro) before nouns.
  • Make possessives agree in number with the thing owned.
  • Talk about how many siblings or pets you have with tener.
  • Ask and answer simple questions about someone's family.

Dialogue

Sofía

¿Cómo es tu familia?

What is your family like?

Mateo

Pequeña. Tengo un hermano y una hermana.

Small. I have one brother and one sister.

Sofía

¿Cómo se llama tu hermano?

What is your brother's name?

Mateo

Mi hermano se llama Diego.

My brother's name is Diego.

Sofía

¿Y tus padres?

And your parents?

Mateo

Mi madre se llama Elena y mi padre, Carlos.

My mother's name is Elena and my father, Carlos.

Sofía

¿Tienes un perro?

Do you have a dog?

Mateo

Sí, nuestro perro se llama Toby.

Yes, our dog's name is Toby.

Sofía

¡Qué bonito! Yo tengo dos gatos.

How nice! I have two cats.

Mateo

¿Cómo se llaman tus gatos?

What are your cats' names?

Sofía

Mis gatos se llaman Luna y Sol.

My cats' names are Luna and Sol.

Grammar

Possessive adjectives

A possessive adjective tells you whose something is. It goes before the noun, just like English 'my' or 'your': mi madre = my mother.

The key rule for A1: most possessives agree with the thing owned, not with the owner. If the noun is plural, add -s.

OwnerOne thingMany things
mymi hermanomis hermanos
your (tú)tu hermanotus hermanos
his / her / your (usted)su hermanosus hermanos
ournuestro hermanonuestros hermanos

mi, tu, su do not change for masculine or feminine — only for singular vs. plural: mi padre, mi madre, mis padres.

nuestro (our) is special: it changes for both gender and number — nuestro perro, nuestra casa, nuestros hijos, nuestras hijas.

The verb tener (to have)

Use tener to say what family or pets you have. It is irregular, so learn these forms:

Pronountener
yotengo
tútienes
él / ella / ustedtiene
nosotrostenemos

Example: Tengo dos hermanas. = I have two sisters.

Pronunciation tip

The double r: perro (dog) has a rolled rr — let your tongue flutter. A single r, as in padre, is just a soft tap. Mixing them up matters: pero means 'but'.

h is silent: hermano, hija, hijo all start with a silent h — say er-MAH-noh, EE-hah, EE-hoh.

ll like 'y': in se llama, the ll sounds like the English 'y': seh YAH-mah.

Culture note

Family is central to daily life across Latin America, and households often include grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins under one roof or living close by. The word familia stretches further than in English — it easily covers the extended family.

You'll also hear warm nicknames: mamá and papá for parents, abuelito/abuelita for grandparents, and mijo/mija (from 'mi hijo/mi hija') used affectionately even with friends.
