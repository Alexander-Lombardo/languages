Health & the Body

What you'll learn

Dialogue

Farmacéutica

Buenas tardes. ¿En qué puedo ayudarle?

Good afternoon. How can I help you?

Marco

Hola. No me siento bien. Me duele la cabeza.

Hi. I don't feel well. My head hurts.

Farmacéutica

¿Tiene fiebre?

Do you have a fever?

Marco

Sí, un poco. Y también me duele la garganta.

Yes, a little. And my throat hurts too.

Farmacéutica

¿Desde cuándo se siente así?

Since when have you felt like this?

Marco

Desde ayer. Creo que tengo un resfriado.

Since yesterday. I think I have a cold.

Farmacéutica

Puede ser. ¿Tiene tos?

It could be. Do you have a cough?

Marco

Sí, por la noche.

Yes, at night.

Farmacéutica

Tome esta medicina dos veces al día.

Take this medicine twice a day.

Marco

¿Necesito una receta?

Do I need a prescription?

Farmacéutica

No, no la necesita. Y descanse mucho.

No, you don't need one. And rest a lot.

Marco

Muchas gracias. ¡Que tenga buen día!

Thank you very much. Have a good day!

Grammar

Saying what hurts: doler

To talk about pain, Spanish uses the verb doler (to hurt) much like gustar. The body part is the subject, and an indirect object pronoun tells whom it hurts. You usually only need two forms: duele (one thing hurts) and duelen (more than one thing hurts).

PronounOne thing hurtsExample
meme dueleMe duele la cabeza.
tete duele¿Te duele el brazo?
lele dueleLe duele el pie.
nosnos dueleNos duele la espalda.
lesles dueleLes duele la garganta.

If more than one body part hurts, use duelen: Me duelen las piernas. (My legs hurt.)

Use the article, not 'my'

With body parts, Spanish uses the definite article (el, la, los, las), not a possessive. Say Me duele la mano, never mi mano.

Talking about symptoms with tener

  • tener fiebre — to have a fever
  • tener tos — to have a cough
  • tener un resfriado — to have a cold
  • tener dolor de + body part — Tengo dolor de estómago.

Pronunciation tip

The 'g' in garganta and estómago: before a, o, u the 'g' is a hard 'g' like in English go. So garganta = gar-GAHN-tah, with two hard g's.

Stress marks help: estómago carries a written accent, so push on the second syllable: es-TOH-mah-goh. Without that mark you'd guess the wrong syllable.

Culture note

In most of Latin America the farmacia is a first stop for minor health problems. Pharmacists are trained to recommend over-the-counter remedies and can often advise you without a doctor's visit. Many pharmacies also offer free blood-pressure checks and basic injections.

For everyday aches, people commonly say Me siento mal (I feel bad) or No me siento bien (I don't feel well). A warm, polite closing like ¡Que se mejore! (Get well soon!) is very common when someone is sick.
