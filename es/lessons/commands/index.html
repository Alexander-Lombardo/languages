Commands (the Imperative)

What you'll learn

  • Form affirmative tú commands using the third-person singular present.
  • Recognise and use the eight common irregular tú commands.
  • Form negative commands and all usted/ustedes commands with the subjunctive.
  • Attach object and reflexive pronouns to commands correctly.
  • Give instructions, advice and directions in everyday situations.

Dialogue

Marta

Diego, ayúdame con esta receta, por favor.

Diego, help me with this recipe, please.

Diego

Claro. Dime, ¿qué hago primero?

Sure. Tell me, what do I do first?

Marta

Pica la cebolla, pero ten cuidado con el cuchillo.

Chop the onion, but be careful with the knife.

Diego

Listo. ¿Agrego el ajo ahora?

Done. Do I add the garlic now?

Marta

No, no lo agregues todavía. Espera dos minutos.

No, don't add it yet. Wait two minutes.

Diego

Vale. ¿Y pruebo la salsa?

Okay. And do I taste the sauce?

Marta

Sí, pruébala y dime si le falta sal.

Yes, taste it and tell me if it needs salt.

Diego

Le falta un poco. ¿Le pongo más?

It needs a little. Should I add more?

Marta

Ponle solo una pizca. No te apures.

Add just a pinch. Don't rush.

Diego

Tranquila, hazme caso: va a quedar riquísimo.

Relax, trust me: it's going to turn out delicious.

Marta

Eso espero. Ahora baja el fuego y tápala.

I hope so. Now lower the heat and cover it.

Grammar

What the imperative does

The imperative gives commands, instructions and advice: turn left, don't worry, add salt. Spanish has different forms depending on who you are addressing (tú, usted, ustedes) and whether the command is affirmative or negative.

Affirmative tú: the easy one

For an affirmative tú command, just use the he/she form of the present tense.

Everything else uses the subjunctive

For negative tú, and for all usted / ustedes commands (affirmative and negative), take the yo form, drop the -o, and add the 'opposite' vowel: -ar verbs take -e endings, -er/-ir verbs take -a endings.

Pronoun placement

This is the part learners forget. Object and reflexive pronouns attach to the end of an affirmative command but go before a negative one:

  • Pruébala. (Taste it.) → No la pruebes. (Don't taste it.)
  • Dímelo. (Tell it to me.) → No me lo digas. (Don't tell it to me.)
  • Levántate. (Get up.) → No te levantes. (Don't get up.)

When you attach a pronoun and the stress shifts, add a written accent to keep it: pon → ponlo, but pruéba → pruébala.

Pronunciation tip

Watch the accent on attached pronouns. Adding a pronoun makes the word longer, so to keep the original stress you often need a written accent: dime (no accent, two syllables) but dímelo (accent needed, three syllables). Say the verb first, then glue the pronouns on without changing where you push.

Tone matters. A bare command can sound blunt. Soften it with por favor, a name, or a friendly ¿sí? at the end: Pásame la sal, ¿sí?

Reading: Cómo llegar a la plaza

Para llegar a la plaza, sal de la estación y dobla a la derecha. Sigue derecho tres cuadras y no cruces la avenida grande. En la esquina del banco, gira a la izquierda y camina media cuadra más. Si te pierdes, pregúntale a alguien o llámame de inmediato. Ten cuidado al cruzar y no te apures: la plaza no se mueve.

Translation

To get to the square, leave the station and turn right. Go straight for three blocks and don't cross the big avenue. At the corner by the bank, turn left and walk half a block more. If you get lost, ask someone or call me right away. Be careful when crossing and don't rush: the square isn't going anywhere.

Culture note

In much of Latin America the tú command is everyday and friendly, but tone is carried by warmth, not grammar. A blunt ¡Ven! softens instantly with a name or porfa: Ven, porfa. In formal settings — speaking to an older person, a client, or a stranger — switch to the usted command, which sounds respectful rather than distant.

Note one regional twist: in Argentina, Uruguay and parts of Central America, people use vos instead of tú, and its affirmative command stresses the last syllable — hablá, comé, vení — instead of habla, come, ven. You'll be understood everywhere with the standard forms taught here.

Talk about it

  • ¿Qué consejo le darías a alguien que visita tu ciudad por primera vez? Usa tres mandatos. — What advice would you give someone visiting your city for the first time? Use three commands.
  • Explica cómo preparar tu plato favorito usando mandatos (pica, agrega, espera...). — Explain how to make your favorite dish using commands (chop, add, wait...).
  • ¿Prefieres dar instrucciones o recibirlas? ¿Por qué? — Do you prefer giving instructions or receiving them? Why?
  • Da indicaciones desde tu casa hasta el lugar más cercano para comprar comida. — Give directions from your house to the nearest place to buy food.
