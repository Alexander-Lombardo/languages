The Near Future (ir a + infinitive)

What you'll learn

  • Conjugate the verb ir in the present tense.
  • Form the near future with ir a + infinitive to talk about plans.
  • Use time expressions like mañana and esta noche to say when.
  • Ask and answer questions about future plans.
  • Make simple negative future statements with no.

Dialogue

Marta

¿Qué vas a hacer mañana?

What are you going to do tomorrow?

Diego

Voy a estudiar para el examen.

I'm going to study for the exam.

Marta

¡Qué aburrido! ¿Y esta noche?

How boring! And tonight?

Diego

Esta noche voy a descansar en casa.

Tonight I'm going to rest at home.

Marta

Nosotros vamos a cocinar una pizza.

We're going to cook a pizza.

Diego

¿Ah sí? ¿Quién va a cocinar?

Oh yeah? Who's going to cook?

Marta

Mi hermano. Él cocina muy bien.

My brother. He cooks very well.

Diego

¿Y el fin de semana? ¿Tienes planes?

And the weekend? Do you have plans?

Marta

Sí, voy a viajar a la playa.

Yes, I'm going to travel to the beach.

Diego

¡Qué bien! Yo no voy a hacer nada.

How nice! I'm not going to do anything.

Marta

¿Vas a visitar a tus abuelos?

Are you going to visit your grandparents?

Diego

Quizás la próxima semana.

Maybe next week.

Grammar

The near future: ir a + infinitive

To talk about plans and things that are going to happen soon, Spanish uses a very easy formula — the same one English uses with "going to":

ir (conjugated) + a + infinitive

For example: Voy a comer = "I'm going to eat." The verb ir changes for each person, but the second verb always stays in its plain dictionary form (the infinitive).

The verb ir (to go) in the present

Making it negative

Just put no before the conjugated ir:

  • No voy a estudiar hoy. — I'm not going to study today.
  • Ella no va a viajar. — She's not going to travel.

Adding when

Drop a time expression at the start or end of the sentence: Mañana voy a descansar or Voy a descansar mañana. Both are correct.

Pronunciation tip

The 'v' in voy, vas, va: in Spanish the letter v sounds like a soft English 'b'. So voy is closer to "boy" than to the English "v".

Link the words: voy a blends into one smooth sound — "BOY-ah" — and va a almost becomes a single long "bah". Don't pause between the verb and the a.

Culture note

In everyday Latin-American speech, the ir a + infinitive future is far more common than the "true" future tense (hablaré, comeré). People say Voy a llamar a mi mamá rather than Llamaré a mi mamá — it sounds warmer and more natural. Mastering this one structure lets you talk about almost any plan, so it's worth more than memorizing the full future tense at this stage.
