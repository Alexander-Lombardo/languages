Grammar

Three different se's and a true passive

Spanish has several ways to talk about an action without naming who does it. Choosing the right one is a hallmark of B2 fluency.

1. The true passive: ser + participio

Formed with ser plus a past participle that agrees with the subject. The agent, if mentioned, follows por. It is most common in formal, written or journalistic registers.

  • La ley fue promulgada por el congreso. (The law was enacted by congress.)
  • Los informes serán redactados mañana. (The reports will be drafted tomorrow.)

2. The passive se: se + verb agreeing with the thing

Far more frequent in speech. The verb agrees with the patient (the thing affected), and the agent is normally not expressed.

  • Se vende esta casa. / Se venden estas casas. (This house / these houses for sale.)
  • Se firmaron los acuerdos. (The agreements were signed.)

3. The impersonal se: always singular

Used with intransitive verbs, or with people introduced by a, to mean one / you / people in general. The verb stays singular.

  • En España se cena tarde. (In Spain people eat dinner late.)
  • Se contrató a tres ingenieros. (Three engineers were hired.) — singular because of a.

4. The accidental (involuntary) se

To present an event as unplanned, use se + indirect-object pronoun + verb. The grammar shifts blame away from the person.

  • Se me rompió el vaso. (The glass broke on me / I accidentally broke it.)
  • Se nos olvidaron las llaves. (We forgot the keys.)

Nuance: Rompí el vaso admits fault; se me rompió el vaso frames it as an accident. The verb agrees with the thing that 'did the breaking'.

Pronunciation tip

Linking se + verb: the clitic cluster runs together as one breath group: se me olvidó sounds like se-me-ol-vi-DÓ with no pause. Don't over-stress the pronouns; the stress lands on the verb.

Participle endings: in fue promulgada, keep the final -da crisp and voiced — sloppy endings blur the gender agreement that carries the meaning.

Reading: Cómo se redacta una ley

En la mayoría de los países hispanohablantes, las leyes se redactan en un registro muy formal donde rara vez se menciona al autor. Primero, un proyecto es presentado por un grupo de legisladores ante la cámara correspondiente. Luego se debaten los artículos uno por uno y se proponen enmiendas. Cuando se alcanza un acuerdo, el texto es aprobado por la mayoría y, finalmente, es promulgado por el poder ejecutivo. Conviene notar que en estos documentos se prefiere la voz pasiva precisamente para destacar la norma y no a las personas. Así, se evita atribuir la responsabilidad a un individuo concreto y se da un tono impersonal y objetivo. Una vez publicada, la ley entra en vigor y se considera vigente para todos los ciudadanos. A partir de ese momento, se entiende que nadie puede alegar que la desconocía.

Translation

In most Spanish-speaking countries, laws are drafted in a very formal register where the author is rarely mentioned. First, a bill is presented by a group of legislators before the relevant chamber. Then the articles are debated one by one and amendments are proposed. When an agreement is reached, the text is approved by the majority and, finally, it is enacted by the executive branch. It is worth noting that in these documents the passive voice is preferred precisely to highlight the rule and not the people. Thus, attributing responsibility to a specific individual is avoided and an impersonal, objective tone is given. Once published, the law comes into force and is considered current for all citizens. From that moment on, it is understood that no one can claim they were unaware of it.

Culture note

The preference for the passive and impersonal se in Spanish legal and administrative writing reflects a long civil-law tradition: the norm, not its author, takes center stage. Phrases like se prohíbe, se ruega and se hace saber populate signs, contracts and official notices across Latin America and Spain.

This is why a Spanish letrero reads Se prohíbe fumar rather than naming who forbids it — the rule is presented as impersonal and binding on everyone. English often does the same with the agentless passive (Smoking is prohibited), but Spanish reaches for se far more readily in everyday speech.

Talk about it

  • ¿En qué situaciones te parece más honesto usar la voz pasiva en lugar de nombrar al responsable? — In what situations does the passive voice seem more honest to you than naming the person responsible?
  • ¿Crees que el <em>se</em> accidental sirve para evadir responsabilidades o solo describe lo ocurrido? — Do you think the accidental se serves to dodge responsibility, or does it just describe what happened?
  • Compara cómo se redactan las normativas en tu idioma con el estilo impersonal del español. — Compare how regulations are written in your language with the impersonal style of Spanish.
  • ¿Qué matiz cambia entre decir «rompí el contrato» y «se rompió el contrato»? — What nuance changes between saying 'I broke the contract' and 'the contract got broken'?
  • ¿En qué ámbitos profesionales conviene destacar al agente y en cuáles conviene omitirlo? — In which professional spheres is it advisable to highlight the agent, and in which is it better to omit it?
