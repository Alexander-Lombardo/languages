Grammar

Two verbs, one English word

Spanish has two verbs that both mean to be: ser and estar. They are not interchangeable. The simplest rule: ser is for what something is (identity, essence), and estar is for how or where something is right now (state, location).

When to use SER

  • Identity / name: Soy Marta.
  • Origin: Soy de Colombia.
  • Job: Es médico.
  • Lasting traits: Eres simpática. / Es alto.

When to use ESTAR

  • Location: Estoy en casa. / Está aquí.
  • Feelings / moods: Estoy cansada. / Está contento.
  • Temporary states: ¿Estás ocupada?

Tip: a quick memory aid is DOCTOR for ser (Description, Occupation, Characteristic, Time, Origin, Relationship) and PLACE for estar (Position, Location, Action, Condition, Emotion).

Pronunciation tip

Adjectives agree: with ser and estar, the adjective matches the person. A man says cansado, contento; a woman says cansada, contenta. The ending swaps -o → -a.

Stress: estás and está carry a written accent on the last syllable — push there: es-TAHS, es-TAH. But soy, eres, es have no accent mark.

Culture note

The ser/estar split shapes how Spanish speakers describe people — and it can be subtle. Saying está guapo (estar) means someone looks handsome today, while es guapo (ser) means he is a handsome person. A few adjectives even change meaning: ser aburrido = to be boring, but estar aburrido = to be bored. Beginners are not expected to master every nuance at once — the DOCTOR/PLACE rules will carry you through almost every A1 sentence.
