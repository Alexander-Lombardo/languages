Grammar

Talking about ideas: turning qualities into nouns

Abstract description leans on nominalisation — converting adjectives and clauses into things you can discuss. The most Spanish device is lo + adjective, which names a quality or 'the ... thing about'.

  • Lo intangible es difícil de medir. — The intangible is hard to measure.
  • Lo curioso del caso es que nadie protestó. — The curious thing about the case is that nobody objected.
  • Lo que más me inquieta es la incertidumbre. — What unsettles me most is the uncertainty.

lo + adjective vs. el/la + abstract noun

FormSenseExample
lo + adj.an open, undefined quality ('the ... aspect')lo profundo — the deep side of it

Hedging: the subjunctive of nuance

To describe the abstract honestly you must matizar — qualify how sure you are. Expressions of doubt, value or possibility trigger the subjunctive.

  • No es que sea falso, sino que esté mal planteado. — It's not that it's false, but that it's badly framed.
  • Cabe que la brecha se ensanche. — It's conceivable the gap widens.
  • Por más que lo intentemos, el matiz se pierde. — However much we try, the nuance is lost.

Indicative vs. subjunctive: certainty contrasted

Indicative (asserted)Subjunctive (hedged / valued)
Es evidente que hay un sesgo.No es evidente que haya un sesgo.
Creo que abarca todo.No creo que lo abarque todo.
Sé que existe la añoranza.Dudo que exista tal añoranza.

Use these to sound measured rather than dogmatic — a hallmark of educated register.

Pronunciation tip

Linking across words (sinalefa): in connected speech, a vowel at the end of one word fuses with the vowel beginning the next. lo intangible flows as loin-tan-GI-ble; la añoranza as la-ño-RAN-za. Don't pause between them.

Abstract nouns and stress: long Latinate words keep a single strong beat. incertidumbre → in-cer-ti-DUM-bre; verosimilitud → ve-ro-si-mi-li-TUD (final stress, no accent because it ends in a consonant other than n/s). Resist the English urge to add secondary stresses.

Reading: Las cosas que no se pueden tocar

Hay un terreno del lenguaje que se resiste a las definiciones limpias: el de lo intangible. Cuando intentamos describir la pertenencia, el desasosiego o la añoranza, las palabras parecen abarcar menos de lo que sentimos. No es que el idioma sea pobre, sino que estas nociones viven en los matices, y el matiz se evapora en cuanto lo nombramos. Cada cultura aporta su propia cosmovisión, un trasfondo que tiñe lo que se considera valioso o amenazante. Por eso una misma idea —digamos, el arraigo a la tierra— puede leerse como virtud en un ámbito y como obstáculo en otro. El buen pensador no soslaya esa ambigüedad; la matiza, reconoce el sesgo desde el que habla y acepta cierta incertidumbre. Describir lo abstracto exige discernimiento: saber cuándo una afirmación gana en verosimilitud al admitir lo que ignora. Quizá la cohesión de una sociedad dependa menos de los acuerdos explícitos que de esas brechas que aprendemos a habitar juntos. Lo curioso es que, al hablar de lo que no se toca, terminamos describiéndonos a nosotros mismos.

Translation

There is a region of language that resists clean definitions: that of the intangible. When we try to describe belonging, unease or longing, words seem to encompass less than what we feel. It's not that the language is poor, but that these notions live in the nuances, and nuance evaporates the moment we name it. Each culture contributes its own worldview, a background that tints what is deemed valuable or threatening. That's why a single idea —say, attachment to one's land— can be read as a virtue in one sphere and an obstacle in another. The good thinker does not sidestep that ambiguity; they qualify it, acknowledge the bias they speak from, and accept a degree of uncertainty. Describing the abstract demands discernment: knowing when a claim gains in plausibility by admitting what it does not know. Perhaps the cohesion of a society depends less on explicit agreements than on those gaps we learn to inhabit together. The curious thing is that, in speaking of what cannot be touched, we end up describing ourselves.

Culture note

Spanish has a rich tradition of the ensayo (essay), where writers like Octavio Paz, José Ortega y Gasset and Rosa Montero probe abstract ideas with exactly the hedged, nuanced register this lesson practises. Two near-untranslatable nouns capture this terrain: añoranza, a tender longing for what is absent (cousin to Portuguese saudade), and desasosiego, a restless unease made famous by Fernando Pessoa's Libro del desasosiego. Reaching for these abstract nouns — rather than long descriptive phrases — is a clear marker of advanced, literate Spanish.

Talk about it

  • ¿Crees que ciertas emociones, como la añoranza, solo pueden expresarse plenamente en la lengua materna? Justifica tu postura. — Do you think certain emotions, like longing, can only be fully expressed in one's mother tongue? Justify your stance.
  • ¿Hasta qué punto la cosmovisión de una cultura determina lo que sus hablantes consideran valioso? — To what extent does a culture's worldview determine what its speakers consider valuable?
  • ¿Es posible describir un concepto abstracto sin proyectar en él nuestros propios sesgos? — Is it possible to describe an abstract concept without projecting our own biases onto it?
  • Matiza esta afirmación: "la cohesión social depende de los acuerdos explícitos". ¿Estás de acuerdo? — Qualify this claim: 'social cohesion depends on explicit agreements'. Do you agree?
  • ¿Por qué crees que el arraigo a un lugar se interpreta a veces como virtud y a veces como obstáculo? — Why do you think attachment to a place is sometimes read as a virtue and sometimes as an obstacle?
