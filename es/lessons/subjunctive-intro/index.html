Present Subjunctive: Wishes & Emotions

What you'll learn

  • Understand what the subjunctive mood expresses and why Spanish needs it.
  • Form the present subjunctive of regular -ar, -er and -ir verbs.
  • Recognise the most common irregular subjunctive forms.
  • Use the subjunctive after expressions of wish, hope and emotion with 'que'.

Dialogue

Marta

¿Sabes? Mañana tengo la entrevista de trabajo.

You know what? Tomorrow I have the job interview.

Diego

¡Qué bien! Espero que te vaya muy bien.

How great! I hope it goes really well for you.

Marta

Gracias. Tengo miedo de que me pregunten cosas difíciles.

Thanks. I'm afraid they'll ask me hard things.

Diego

Ojalá te toque un jefe simpático.

Hopefully you get a nice boss.

Marta

Eso espero. Quiero que vean que tengo experiencia.

I hope so. I want them to see that I have experience.

Diego

Me alegra que estés tan preparada.

I'm glad you're so prepared.

Marta

Me molesta que la empresa esté tan lejos, eso sí.

It does bother me that the company is so far away, that's true.

Diego

Bueno, vale la pena que lo intentes.

Well, it's worth it for you to try.

Marta

Tienes razón. Ojalá consiga el puesto.

You're right. I hope I get the position.

Diego

Seguro que sí. Me sorprendería que no te eligieran.

I'm sure you will. I'd be surprised if they didn't choose you.

Grammar

What is the subjunctive?

So far you have used the indicative mood — for facts and certainty: Ella viene (she is coming). The subjunctive is a different mood used for things that are wished for, doubted, or emotionally coloured rather than stated as fact. It almost always appears in a second clause introduced by que.

Compare: Sé que viene (I know she's coming — fact, indicative) vs. Espero que venga (I hope she comes — wish, subjunctive).

Forming the present subjunctive

Take the yo form of the present indicative, drop the -o, and add the opposite vowel endings: -ar verbs take e endings; -er/-ir verbs take a endings.

Common irregulars

A few verbs have irregular subjunctive stems. Memorise these five:

  • ser → sea, seas, sea, seamos, sean
  • estar → esté, estés, esté, estemos, estén
  • ir → vaya, vayas, vaya, vayamos, vayan
  • haber → haya, hayas, haya, hayamos, hayan
  • saber → sepa, sepas, sepa, sepamos, sepan

Triggers: wishes & emotions

When the main clause expresses a wish or an emotion and the subject of the second clause is different, use the subjunctive after que:

  • Espero que tengas suerte. — I hope you have luck.
  • Quiero que vengas conmigo. — I want you to come with me.
  • Me alegra que estés bien. — I'm glad you're well.
  • Temo que llueva mañana. — I'm afraid it'll rain tomorrow.
  • Ojalá llegue a tiempo. — Hopefully it arrives on time. (Ojalá needs no que.)

Same subject? Use the infinitive instead: Quiero ir (I want to go) vs. Quiero que vayas (I want you to go).

Pronunciation tip

Stress stays put: the subjunctive endings keep the stress on the same syllable as the indicative — HA-bla and HA-ble, CO-me and CO-ma. Don't let the new vowel pull your stress forward.

Ojalá is stressed on the final syllable: oh-hah-LAH. The j is that raspy 'h' sound, and the word comes from Arabic law šā' Allāh, 'if God wills'.

Reading: Una carta de ánimo

Querida Lucía: Espero que estés bien y que el nuevo trabajo te guste. Me alegra mucho que hayas tomado esta decisión. Ojalá que tus compañeros sean amables y que aprendas cosas nuevas cada día. Temo que al principio todo sea un poco difícil, pero quiero que sepas que vale la pena. Deseo que logres todos tus objetivos y que mejores tu salud con el cambio.

Translation

Dear Lucía: I hope you're well and that you like the new job. I'm very glad that you've made this decision. Hopefully your coworkers are kind and you learn new things every day. I'm afraid that at first everything will be a bit hard, but I want you to know that it's worth it. I wish that you achieve all your goals and that you improve your health with the change.

Culture note

The subjunctive is one of the features that most distinguishes fluent speakers from learners, and Spanish uses it far more than English. English mostly buried its subjunctive (we keep traces in 'I wish I were...' or 'I suggest he be...'), but in Spanish it is alive and everywhere — in wishes, doubts, requests and emotions.

The everyday word ojalá is a small piece of history: it entered Spanish from Arabic during the centuries of Al-Ándalus and literally meant 'may God will it.' Today people across Latin America say it dozens of times a day without a thought for its origin — a reminder that Spanish carries roughly four thousand words of Arabic heritage.

Talk about it

  • ¿Qué deseas que pase este año en tu vida? — What do you wish happens this year in your life?
  • ¿Qué cosa te molesta que la gente haga en el trabajo? — What thing bothers you that people do at work?
  • ¿De qué tienes miedo que ocurra en el futuro? — What are you afraid might happen in the future?
  • ¿Qué esperas que aprendan tus amigos de ti? — What do you hope your friends learn from you?
