Grammar

What the passato remoto is — and why you read it

The passato remoto is a simple (one-word) past tense that narrates completed, self-contained actions. In most of central and northern speech it has been replaced by the passato prossimo (ho visto), but it dominates written narrative: novels, short stories, fairy tales, history and journalism. For a reader at C1 the priority is recognition, not production — you must be able to look at vide, disse or giunse and instantly read “saw”, “said”, “arrived”. In the south (especially Sicily) it is still alive in speech, even for recent events.

The irregulars: the “1-3-3” pattern

Most irregular verbs follow one rule of thumb: only io, lui/lei and loro are irregular (they share a special stem and endings -i / -e / -ero); tu, noi, voi stay regular. So once you know vidi you can predict vide and videro:

Reading it in context: tense interplay

In narrative the passato remoto carries the foreground events one after another (giunse… vide… disse), while the imperfetto paints the background — descriptions, states, habits (il sole calava, era stanco). Earlier actions appear in the trapassato prossimo (aveva lasciato, aveva attraversato). Recognising this division lets you follow the timeline of any story without translating word by word.

Pronunciation tip

Listen for the stress: the third-person singular of -are verbs is end-stressed and written with a grave accent — par-LÒ, an-DÒ, cal-Ò. By contrast the noi forms double the consonant and stress the antepenult: par-LAM-mo, cre-DEM-mo. Don’t confuse irregular fece (FE-ce, “did/made”) with the regular present fa, or venne (VEN-ne, “came”) with viene (“comes”). The doubled consonants in ebbe, venne, volle, seppe are held audibly long — that length is your clue that you’re reading the passato remoto.

Reading: Il ritorno del viandante

Allorché il sole calò dietro le colline, il viandante giunse al villaggio che aveva lasciato vent’anni prima. Si fermò sulla soglia della vecchia casa e per un istante non riconobbe nulla. Indi vide la quercia, la stessa che da bambino aveva scalato, e il cuore gli balzò in petto.

Una donna uscì sull’aia. Lo guardò a lungo, poi disse soltanto il suo nome. Egli non rispose; fece un passo avanti e le prese le mani. «Sei tornato», mormorò lei, e furono le uniche parole che pronunciarono per molto tempo.

Quella notte il viandante non chiuse occhio. Rammentò i volti che il fato aveva disperso, le promesse che non aveva mantenuto, i mari che aveva attraversato. All’alba si alzò, scese in cortile e udì il canto di un gallo. Capì allora che era davvero a casa, e che lì sarebbe rimasto.

Translation

When the sun set behind the hills, the wayfarer reached the village he had left twenty years before. He stopped on the threshold of the old house and for a moment recognised nothing. Then he saw the oak, the very one he had climbed as a child, and his heart leapt in his chest.

A woman came out onto the farmyard. She looked at him for a long while, then said only his name. He did not answer; he took a step forward and took her hands. “You’ve come back,” she murmured, and those were the only words they spoke for a long time.

That night the wayfarer did not sleep a wink. He recalled the faces that fate had scattered, the promises he had not kept, the seas he had crossed. At dawn he rose, went down into the courtyard and heard a rooster crow. He understood then that he was truly home, and that there he would remain.

Culture note

The passato remoto is at the heart of Italy’s literary tradition: Boccaccio’s Decameron, Manzoni’s I promessi sposi — the novel every Italian student dissects in school — and the great twentieth-century novels of Calvino, Morante and Tomasi di Lampedusa (Il Gattopardo) all unfold in it. Geography matters too: a Milanese might never use the tense in conversation, saying «ieri sono andato», while a Sicilian may quite naturally say «ieri andai» even for yesterday. So the same form is, at once, the most bookish of tenses up north and a living part of everyday speech in the south.

Talk about it

  • Perché, secondo te, la lingua scritta conserva il passato remoto mentre il parlato lo abbandona? _(Why do you think written language keeps the passato remoto while speech abandons it?)_
  • Quale effetto crea l’uso del passato remoto in questo brano rispetto a un racconto scritto al passato prossimo? _(What effect does the passato remoto create here compared with a story told in the passato prossimo?)_
  • Hai notato l’alternanza tra passato remoto e imperfetto: come ti aiuta a seguire la vicenda? _(You noticed the alternation between passato remoto and imperfetto: how does it help you follow the story?)_
  • Parole come «indi», «allorché» e «udire» appartengono a un registro alto. Quando un autore sceglie di usarle, cosa comunica al lettore? _(Words like “indi”, “allorché” and “udire” belong to a high register. When an author chooses them, what do they signal to the reader?)_
  • Qual è l’ultimo romanzo o racconto italiano che hai letto (o vorresti leggere)? Ti spaventa di più il vocabolario o i tempi verbali? _(What is the last Italian novel or story you read, or would like to? Does the vocabulary or the verb tenses worry you more?)_
