Commands (the Imperative)

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Form the informal imperative for tu, noi and voi (incl. -are verbs: parla!).
  • Make negative tu commands with non + the infinitive (non parlare!).
  • Use the formal Lei imperative (Scusi, Senta, Prenda) built from the subjunctive.
  • Place pronouns: attached to tu/noi/voi forms (dimmi, fallo) but before the Lei form.

Dialogue

Cliente

Scusi, mi dica: dov’è la stazione più vicina?

Excuse me, tell me: where's the nearest station?

Vigile

Allora, vada sempre dritto e poi giri a destra.

So, go straight ahead and then turn right.

Cliente

Va bene. È lontano? Devo prendere l’autobus?

All right. Is it far? Should I take the bus?

Vigile

Non si preoccupi, sono cinque minuti a piedi. Prenda Via Roma.

Don't worry, it's a five-minute walk. Take Via Roma.

Marco

Anna, dai, sbrigati! Il treno parte tra dieci minuti.

Anna, come on, hurry up! The train leaves in ten minutes.

Anna

Aspetta un attimo! Dimmi, hai preso tu i biglietti?

Wait a second! Tell me, did you get the tickets?

Marco

Sì, fidati. Non correre, c’è ancora tempo.

Yes, trust me. Don't run, there's still time.

Anna

Allora compriamo un caffè. Senti, prendi anche un’acqua per me?

Then let's buy a coffee. Hey, will you get a water for me too?

Marco

Certo, fallo tu il caffè mentre io vado in biglietteria.

Sure, you make the coffee while I go to the ticket office.

Anna

D’accordo. Ragazzi, venite, andiamo verso il binario tre!

Okay. Guys, come on, let's head to platform three!

Grammar

The informal imperative: tu, noi, voi

The imperative gives orders, instructions and advice. Informally you have three forms. The big trap is the tu of -are verbs: it ends in -a (not -i, which is the present tense). The noi and voi forms are identical to the present tense.

parlare (-are)prendere (-ere)dormire (-ire)
tuparla!prendi!dormi!
noiparliamo!prendiamo!dormiamo!
voiparlate!prendete!dormite!

noi = ‘let’s …’ (parliamo! = let’s talk!); voi addresses a group. Compare Tu parli (you talk — statement) with Parla! (talk! — command).

Saying ‘don’t’: the negative imperative

For a negative tu command, use non + the infinitive — never the bare command form. For noi and voi, just add non in front of the normal form.

  • tu: Non parlare! (Don’t talk!) · Non correre! (Don’t run!)
  • noi: Non parliamo! (Let’s not talk!)
  • voi: Non parlate! (Don’t talk! — to a group)

The formal imperative: Lei (Scusi, Senta, Prenda)

To give a polite order to one person you address with Lei, use the subjunctive form. For most verbs: -are → -i, -ere / -ire → -a. The negative simply adds non (the infinitive trick does NOT apply here).

Irregular Lei forms worth knowing: vada! (go!), faccia! (do!), dia! (give!), stia! (stay!), venga! (come!), abbia pazienza! (be patient!), sia gentile! (be kind!). Negative: Non si preoccupi! (Don’t worry!).

Irregular tu forms and attaching pronouns

Five common verbs have a short, apostrophe tu form (the full form in brackets is also fine): va’ (vai), da’ (dai), fa’ (fai), sta’ (stai), di’ (only this). Also abbi! (have/be) and sii! (be) from avere/essere.

Object/reflexive pronouns attach to the end of tu, noi and voi forms: dammi! (give me), parlagli! (talk to him), alzati! (get up), fallo! (do it). After the five short forms the first consonant of the pronoun doubles (except with gli): da’ + mi → dammi, di’ + mi → dimmi, fa’ + lo → fallo, sta’ + ci → stacci, but da’ + gli → dagli.

With the formal Lei, pronouns go before the verb: mi dica! (tell me!), si accomodi! (make yourself comfortable!), lo prenda! (take it!). In a negative tu command you may put the pronoun before or after: Non lo fare! = Non farlo!

Pronunciation tip

When a pronoun attaches and the consonant doubles (dimmi, dammi, fallo), really hold that double consonant — say dim-mi, not di-mi. The length is meaningful in Italian. Also, the apostrophe in va’, fa’, di’ marks a dropped syllable, so the vowel stays short and clipped: fa’ sounds just like the note ‘fa’.

Culture note

You’ll hear Senta! and Scusi! constantly in Italy — they’re the polite way to flag down a waiter, a shop assistant or a stranger, both built from the formal Lei imperative. Note the social code: a barista or shopkeeper may address a young customer with the friendly tu (Dimmi!, ‘what’ll it be?’), but you should reply with Lei (Mi dica… Prenda pure) until invited to switch. The everyday filler dai! — literally ‘give!’ — has drifted far from its verb to mean ‘come on!’, ‘no way!’ or ‘please!’ depending on the tone.

Talk about it

  • Quando chiedi un’informazione per strada, usi il «tu» o il «Lei»? _(When you ask for directions in the street, do you use ‘tu’ or ‘Lei’?)_
  • Dai spesso consigli ai tuoi amici? Fammi un esempio. _(Do you often give advice to your friends? Give me an example.)_
  • Qual è un ordine che senti spesso in famiglia, tipo «sbrigati!»? _(What’s a command you often hear at home, like ‘hurry up!’?)_
  • Se un turista ti chiede aiuto, come gli rispondi gentilmente? _(If a tourist asks you for help, how do you reply politely?)_
  • Preferisci dare istruzioni con «per favore» o con l’imperativo secco? Perché? _(Do you prefer to give instructions with ‘please’ or with a blunt imperative? Why?)_
