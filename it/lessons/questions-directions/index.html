Dialogue

Turista

Scusi, dov’è la stazione?

Excuse me, where is the station?

Signora

È vicino, a cinque minuti.

It's near, five minutes away.

Turista

Come ci arrivo?

How do I get there?

Signora

Vada sempre dritto fino al semaforo.

Go straight ahead to the traffic light.

Turista

E poi?

And then?

Signora

Poi giri a destra. C’è una banca all’angolo.

Then turn right. There's a bank on the corner.

Turista

C’è anche un bar lì vicino?

Is there a bar near there too?

Signora

Sì, ci sono due bar nella piazza.

Yes, there are two bars in the square.

Turista

Grazie mille, molto gentile!

Thank you very much, very kind!

Signora

Prego, buona giornata!

You're welcome, have a good day!

Grammar

Question words

Most Italian questions begin with a question word. The word order stays the same as a statement — you just add the question word and a rising tone (and a ?).

ItalianEnglishExample
chiwhoChi è? (Who is it?)
che / cosa / che cosawhatCosa fai? (What are you doing?)
dovewhereDove abiti? (Where do you live?)
quandowhenQuando arrivi? (When do you arrive?)
perchéwhy / becausePerché studi? (Why do you study?)
comehowCome stai? (How are you?)
quantohow muchQuanto costa? (How much does it cost?)
qualewhichQuale vuoi? (Which one do you want?)

Two handy contractions: dove + è → dov’è (Dov’è il bagno?) and come + è → com’è (Com’è la casa?). Note that perché means both why and because — the answer reuses the same word: Perché non vieni? — Perché sono stanco.

Quanto and quale agree

Unlike the others, quanto and quale change to match the noun. Quanto has four forms; quale has two and shortens to qual before è (with no apostrophe): Qual è il problema?

Giving directions

For a polite stranger you use the formal command of the verb: vada (go), giri (turn), prenda (take), attraversi (cross). Pair them with these direction words:

  • a destra — to/on the right
  • a sinistra — to/on the left
  • sempre dritto — straight ahead
  • vicino (a) — near (to) · lontano (da) — far (from)
  • all’angolo — on the corner · fino a — as far as / up to

Example: Giri a sinistra e vada sempre dritto fino alla piazza. La banca è a destra.

C’è and ci sono

To say there is / there are, use c’è with a singular thing and ci sono with a plural. They tell you what exists or is present in a place.

NumberItalianEnglish
singularC’è un bar in piazza.There is a bar in the square.
pluralCi sono due banche qui.There are two banks here.
questionC’è una farmacia vicino?Is there a pharmacy nearby?
negativeNon c’è una stazione qui.There isn't a station here.

C’è is simply ci (there) + è (is), so it always keeps the apostrophe and the accent.

Pronunciation tip

The apostrophe in c’è and dov’è: the apostrophe shows a dropped vowel, but you still pronounce both parts smoothly as one word — c’è is "cheh" and dov’è is "doh-VEH". Don't confuse c’è (there is, with accent) with che (that/what, no accent): they sound almost the same but mean different things.

Stress on question words: perché carries a written accent on the final -é, so the voice rises at the end: pehr-KEH. The others stress the first syllable: DOH-veh, KWAN-doh, KWAH-leh.

Culture note

In Italian towns the piazza is the heart of public life — the square in front of the church or town hall where people meet, take an evening stroll (the passeggiata) and sit at a café. When you ask for directions, locals often point you toward a piazza or a church as a landmark rather than giving street names, since historic centres have winding medieval streets. A polite opener is Scusi for a stranger (formal) or Scusa for someone your own age (informal), and a quick Grazie, molto gentile! at the end is always appreciated.
