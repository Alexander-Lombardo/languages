Family & Possessives

Dialogue

Lucia

Questa è una foto della mia famiglia.

This is a photo of my family.

Davide

Che bella! È tua madre, la signora a sinistra?

How nice! Is your mother the lady on the left?

Lucia

Sì, è mia madre. E accanto a lei c’è mio padre.

Yes, that’s my mother. And next to her is my father.

Davide

E questi due ragazzi? Sono i tuoi fratelli?

And these two boys? Are they your brothers?

Lucia

Sono i miei fratelli, Marco e Luca. Non ho sorelle.

They’re my brothers, Marco and Luca. I don’t have sisters.

Davide

Anche la mia mamma è in questa foto? Scherzo!

Is my mom in this photo too? Just kidding!

Lucia

Ah ah! No, ma c’è la mia nonna, la signora anziana.

Ha ha! No, but my grandmother is there, the elderly lady.

Davide

La tua è una famiglia molto simpatica.

Yours is a very nice family.

Lucia

Grazie! Domenica pranziamo tutti insieme. Vieni anche tu?

Thanks! On Sunday we all have lunch together. Will you come too?

Grammar

Possessive adjectives: the four forms

An Italian possessive agrees in gender and number with the thing owned, not with the owner. Each possessive therefore has four forms, and it is normally used with the definite article: il / la / i / le.

Ownermasc. sing.fem. sing.masc. plur.fem. plur.
myil miola miai mieile mie
your (sing.)il tuola tuai tuoile tue
his / her / your (formal)il suola suai suoile sue
ouril nostrola nostrai nostrile nostre
your (plur.)il vostrola vostrai vostrile vostre
theiril lorola loroi lorole loro

Notice that loro never changes ending — only the article in front of it changes: il loro figlio, la loro figlia, i loro figli, le loro figlie. With everything except loro, the possessive matches the noun: la mia casa (fem. sing.), i miei libri (masc. plur.).

The family rule: drop the article

There is one big exception. With a singular, unmodified family member, you drop the article before the possessive:

  • mia madre — my mother (not la mia madre)
  • tuo padre — your father
  • suo fratello — his/her brother
  • nostra sorella — our sister

This works for close relatives: madre, padre, fratello, sorella, figlio, figlia, marito, moglie, nonno, nonna, zio, zia, cugino, cugina.

When the article comes back

The article returns whenever the family noun is plural, altered/affectionate, modified by an adjective, or used with loro:

No articleArticle requiredWhy
mia sorellale mie sorelleplural
mia madrela mia mammaaffectionate form (mamma, papà)
tuo fratelloil tuo fratello maggioremodified by an adjective
sua figliala loro figliaowner is loro

So: mio fratello but i miei fratelli; mia madre but la mia mamma and la mia cara madre.

Pronunciation tip

In figlio, figlia, moglie, famiglia the cluster gli/gl is a soft “ly” sound made with the tongue against the palate — like the lli in English “million,” never a hard g. The g in gn-words such as signora is the same family of palatal sounds: signora = “see-NYÓ-ra.”

Culture note

Family is central to Italian life, and the pranzo della domenica — the long Sunday lunch — is a cherished ritual where several generations gather around the table. The figure of the nonna (grandmother) is especially beloved: she is often the keeper of family recipes, and many Italians insist that no one’s ragù or lasagne is ever as good as their grandmother’s.
