What you'll learn

  • Use everyday idioms naturally: in bocca al lupo, non vedo l’ora, avere voglia di, valere la pena.
  • Link ideas with cause-and-result connectors: quindi, perciò, siccome.
  • Express contrast and concession with però, invece, anche se.
  • Show simultaneity and opposition with mentre, and choose the right connector for the meaning.

Dialogue

Giulia

Allora, domani hai l’esame di guida. Non vedo l’ora di sapere com’è andata!

So, tomorrow you have your driving test. I can’t wait to hear how it goes!

Marco

Sono nervoso, però mi sono preparato bene. Penso che ce la faccio.

I’m nervous, but I’ve prepared well. I think I can do it.

Giulia

Certo che ce la fai. Siccome hai fatto tante ore di pratica, sei pronto.

Of course you can. Since you’ve done so many hours of practice, you’re ready.

Marco

Speriamo. Anche se conosco le regole, ho sempre paura del parcheggio.

Let’s hope so. Even though I know the rules, I’m always scared of parking.

Giulia

Il parcheggio è solo questione di calma. Quindi domani respira e vai piano.

Parking is just a matter of staying calm. So tomorrow breathe and go slowly.

Marco

Hai ragione. Mentre tu sei tranquilla, io invece mi agito per niente.

You’re right. While you’re calm, I, on the other hand, get worked up over nothing.

Giulia

Vale la pena fare un ultimo giro di prova stasera? Avrei voglia di aiutarti.

Is it worth doing one last practice round tonight? I’d feel like helping you.

Marco

Magari! Perciò ti chiamo dopo cena e usciamo con la macchina di mio padre.

I’d love that! So I’ll call you after dinner and we’ll go out with my dad’s car.

Giulia

Perfetto. In fin dei conti un po’ di pratica in più non fa mai male.

Perfect. When all is said and done, a bit of extra practice never hurts.

Marco

Grazie davvero. Senza di te sarei molto più ansioso.

Thanks, really. Without you I’d be much more anxious.

Giulia

Figurati! Allora a stasera, e per domani… in bocca al lupo!

Don’t mention it! See you tonight then, and for tomorrow… good luck!

Marco

Crepi il lupo! Domani a quest’ora avrò la patente in tasca.

Thanks (lit. “may the wolf die”)! By this time tomorrow I’ll have my licence in my pocket.

Grammar

Everyday idioms you’ll hear daily

Idioms can’t be translated word for word — you learn the whole phrase. Note how each one connects to the rest of the sentence:

IdiomMeaningHow it’s used
in bocca al lupogood luckYou wish it; the other person replies crepi (il lupo)! Never answer grazie — it’s bad luck.
non vedo l’ora (di + infinitive)I can’t wait“Non vedo l’ora di partire.” / “Non vedo l’ora che arrivi l’estate.”
avere voglia di (+ noun/infinitive)to feel like“Ho voglia di un caffè.” / “Non ho voglia di uscire.”
valere la pena (+ infinitive)to be worth it“Vale la pena provare.” / “Non ne vale la pena.”

Notice that avere voglia di, non vedo l’ora di and valere la pena are all followed by di + infinitive (or just an infinitive for valere la pena). The idiom farcela (“to manage it”) keeps its pronouns: ce la faccio, ce la fai, non ce l’ho fatta.

Connectors of cause and result

These join two ideas where one explains or follows from the other. Siccome introduces the cause and usually opens the sentence; quindi and perciò introduce the result.

ConnectorMeansExample
siccomesince / as (cause, at the start)Siccome piove, restiamo a casa.
quindiso / therefore (result)Piove, quindi restiamo a casa.
perciòtherefore / for this reason (result)Piove, perciò restiamo a casa.

Quindi and perciò are almost interchangeable; perciò sounds a touch more formal. Both come after the cause. Siccome answers “why?” and comes before the result — you would not start a sentence with perché in this way.

Connectors of contrast and time

Use these to oppose ideas or set them side by side:

  • però = but / however — joins two clauses: “Mi piace, però costa troppo.”
  • invece = instead / on the other hand — swaps one thing for another: “Non prendo il dolce; invece prendo un caffè.”
  • anche se = even if / even though — concession: “Esco anche se piove.”
  • mentre = while (same time) or whereas (contrast): “Mentre cucino, ascolto la musica.” / “Lui è calmo, mentre io mi agito.”

Però vs ma: both mean “but,” but però can also be added at the end for emphasis (“È caro, però!” = “It’s pricey, though!”), where ma cannot. Invece stresses a switch or surprise, so it often pairs nicely with a preceding negative.

Pronunciation tip

perciò and però carry their stress on the final, accented vowel: per-CHOH, peh-ROH. The written accent (ò) tells you exactly where to land your voice — don’t flatten it onto the first syllable. Compare the unaccented siccome (sik-KOH-meh) and mentre (MEN-treh), which are stressed earlier. In perciò the ci is the soft “ch” of church, so the whole word is per-CHOH, not “per-see-oh.”

Culture note

The most famous Italian good-luck wish is “in bocca al lupo” — literally “into the wolf’s mouth.” It probably comes from hunters and shepherds, the wolf being the great danger of the countryside; wishing someone into its jaws was an ironic charm against misfortune. The expected reply is “crepi il lupo!” (“may the wolf die!”), and Italians will tell you that answering plain grazie jinxes it. You’ll hear it everywhere before exams, job interviews, and performances. A gentler modern variant, “in culo alla balena” – “viva la balena!”, is more playful and informal, but in bocca al lupo remains the classic.

Talk about it

  • Di cosa hai voglia in questo momento? _(What do you feel like right now?)_
  • C’è qualcosa che non vedi l’ora di fare quest’anno? _(Is there something you can’t wait to do this year?)_
  • Secondo te, vale la pena imparare l’italiano? Perché? _(In your opinion, is it worth learning Italian? Why?)_
  • Racconta una situazione in cui ce l’hai fatta anche se era difficile. _(Tell about a time you managed it even though it was hard.)_
  • Tu sei più calmo o più ansioso prima di un esame? E i tuoi amici, invece? _(Are you calmer or more anxious before an exam? And your friends, on the other hand?)_
