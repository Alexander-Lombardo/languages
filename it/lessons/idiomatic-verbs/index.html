Grammar

What is a pronominal verb?

A verbo pronominale is a verb that has fused with one or two clitic pronouns — usually ci/ce, ne, and the feminine la — to form a new lexical unit with its own meaning. Fare means “to do”, but farcela (fare + ci + la) means “to manage, to make it.” The clitics no longer point to anything concrete: they are simply baked into the verb.

When clitics combine, ci becomes ce and the indirect-object pronouns (mi, ti, si, vi) become me, te, se, ve before ne or la. So mi + ne → me ne, ci + la → ce la.

Note how only the reflexive part changes with the subject (me / te / se / ce / ve / se), while the other clitic (la or ne) stays fixed.

The passato prossimo: auxiliary and agreement

Because the reflexive clitic is present, these verbs take essere in compound tenses. The past participle agrees with the fixed clitic:

  • With -la, the participle ends in -a: Ce l’ho fatta! (I made it!), Te la sei cavata bene.
  • With -ne, the participle agrees with the subject: Maria se n’è andata; i ragazzi se ne sono andati.

So: se ne va → se n’è andato/a; ce la faccio → ce l’ho fatta; me la cavo → me la sono cavata.

In the imperative and with infinitives the clitics attach to the end: Vattene! (Get out!), Smettila!, Devo andarmene.

Pronunciation tip

These fused clitics are pronounced as a single quick run-up to the verb, with no pause: me-ne-VA-do, ce-la-FAC-cio — the stress lands on the verb, not the pronouns. Watch the elisions in writing and speech: before ho/hai/ha the la elides to l’ (ce l’ho fatta, ce l’hai con me), and se ne + è becomes se n’è. The double cc in faccio and the soft c in cavo stay crisp even at speed.

Reading: Ce la facciamo?

«Non ce la faccio più con questo lavoro», sbuffò Giulia mentre chiudeva il computer. «Me ne andrei subito, ma non so se me la caverei senza uno stipendio fisso.» Il fratello la guardò: «Non te la prendere, sei brava. Se ci metti un po’ di pazienza, ce la fai di sicuro.»

«Il problema è il capo», continuò lei. «Ce l’ha con me da settimane e io non ne posso più. Una parte di me se ne fregherebbe e se la godrebbe altrove, ma un’altra parte vuole solo che lui la smetta di criticarmi.»

«Senti», disse il fratello, «tu te ne intendi del tuo mestiere più di chiunque altro. L’anno scorso te la sei vista brutta con quel progetto e ce l’hai fatta lo stesso. Metticela tutta ancora un mese, poi decidi con calma se restare o andartene.» Giulia sorrise: forse aveva ragione.

Translation

“I can’t take this job anymore,” huffed Giulia as she closed her laptop. “I’d leave right now, but I don’t know if I’d get by without a steady salary.” Her brother looked at her: “Don’t take it so hard, you’re good. If you put in a bit of patience, you’ll definitely make it.”

“The problem is the boss,” she went on. “He’s had it in for me for weeks and I can’t stand it anymore. Part of me wouldn’t give a damn and would go enjoy life elsewhere, but another part just wants him to quit criticising me.”

“Listen,” said her brother, “you know your trade better than anyone. Last year you had a rough time with that project and you made it through anyway. Give it your all for one more month, then calmly decide whether to stay or to leave.” Giulia smiled: maybe he was right.

Culture note

Pronominal verbs are the heartbeat of everyday spoken Italian, and many have become cultural shorthand. The most famous is the title of Roberto Benigni and Massimo Troisi’s 1983 film Non ci resta che piangere (“There’s nothing left for us but to cry”), built on the pronominal restarci. The blunt fregarsene even spawned the noun menefreghismo — a couldn’t-care-less attitude — once associated with Fascist-era slogans and still used today to scold political apathy. Mastering these verbs is less about grammar than about sounding like a real Italian rather than a textbook.

Talk about it

  • C’è una situazione difficile in cui alla fine «ce l’hai fatta»? Racconta come te la sei cavata. _(Is there a hard situation you ultimately “made it” through? Tell how you got by.)_
  • Quando qualcuno ti critica, te la prendi facilmente o te ne freghi? _(When someone criticises you, do you take offence easily or not care?)_
  • C’è un argomento di cui «te ne intendi» davvero? Come sei diventato esperto? _(Is there a topic you really know a lot about? How did you become an expert?)_
  • In che momenti hai voglia di «andartene» e ricominciare altrove? _(In what moments do you feel like leaving and starting over elsewhere?)_
  • Perché secondo te l’italiano colloquiale usa così tanti verbi pronominali al posto di parole più semplici? _(Why do you think colloquial Italian uses so many pronominal verbs instead of simpler words?)_
