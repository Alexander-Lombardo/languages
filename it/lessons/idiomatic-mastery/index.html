Grammar

Idioms vs. proverbs: two kinds of fixed expression

A modo di dire (idiom) is a fixed multi-word phrase whose meaning cannot be deduced from its parts: tagliare la corda has nothing to do with rope. A proverbio (proverb) is a complete, free-standing sentence that states a piece of folk wisdom: non tutte le ciambelle riescono col buco. The crucial C2 skill is not just understanding these in senso figurato but slotting them in with the right collocations, verb tense and register — using them alla lettera or out of context marks you instantly as a non-native.

The five core expressions and how they behave

Idioms inflect like ordinary phrases: the verb conjugates and any pronoun agrees, but the rest of the phrase is frozen. You say ho tagliato la corda, never «ho tagliato le corde».

Note that di punto in bianco is an adverbial idiom: it modifies a verb and never changes form, unlike the four that contain a conjugating verb.

Register and the danger of the cliché

Most of these belong to colloquial but educated speech and informal writing. A well-placed idiom is azzeccato and calzante; a tired one, dropped into a formal report, sounds fuori luogo, logoro o abusato. The art is restraint: one vivid expression that coglie nel segno and rende l’idea is worth more than three predictable ones strung together.

Pronunciation tip

Idioms are delivered as a single rhythmic unit, almost without internal pauses — that flow is half of what makes them sound native. In di punto in bianco the phrase rushes to the stressed BIAN-co: di-pun-toin-BIAN-co, with punto in running together. In prendere due piccioni con una fava note the double consonant of pic-CIO-ni (held long) and the soft c of ciambelle (CHAM, like English “ch”). Resist the urge to stress each word equally: let the unstressed syllables shrink and the phrase will land like a single brushstroke.

Reading: Le mani in pasta

Quando Marta annunciò di punto in bianco che lasciava lo studio legale per aprire una piccola casa editrice, nessuno dei colleghi le credette. «Non tutte le ciambelle riescono col buco», le disse il socio più anziano, scuotendo la testa. Lei sorrise e non rispose: aveva già le mani in pasta da mesi.

In realtà Marta aveva saputo prendere due piccioni con una fava. Mentre tutti la immaginavano a fare l’avvocato, lei aveva stretto accordi con un paio di tipografie e si era costruita una rete di traduttori. Il giorno in cui consegnò la lettera di dimissioni, tagliò la corda con un sorriso, senza rimpianti.

Il primo anno fu durissimo. Più di un libro andò storto, e ci furono notti in cui pensò di aver buttato all’aria una carriera sicura. Ma a poco a poco i conti tornarono. «Vedi» disse un giorno al socio che l’aveva avvertita, «non tutte le ciambelle riescono col buco, è vero. Però ogni tanto qualcuna riesce.» E quella, almeno, era riuscita benissimo.

Translation

When Marta announced out of the blue that she was leaving the law firm to open a small publishing house, none of her colleagues believed her. “You can’t win them all,” the most senior partner told her, shaking his head. She smiled and said nothing: she had already had her hands in the dough for months.

In fact Marta had managed to kill two birds with one stone. While everyone pictured her practising law, she had struck deals with a couple of printers and built up a network of translators. The day she handed in her resignation letter, she slipped away with a smile, without regrets.

The first year was brutally hard. More than one book went wrong, and there were nights when she thought she had thrown a secure career away. But little by little the books balanced. “You see,” she said one day to the partner who had warned her, “it’s true you can’t win them all. But every now and then one comes out right.” And that one, at least, had come out beautifully.

Culture note

Many of Italy’s most vivid sayings are rooted in la cucina and the rhythms of rural life: non tutte le ciambelle riescono col buco recalls the home baker whose ring-shaped doughnut doesn’t always come out perfectly holed, while prendere due piccioni con una fava evokes old hunting and farmyard imagery (a single bean luring two birds). This earthy, food-and-fields imagery is no accident — for centuries proverbs were the everyday philosophy of a largely agrarian, regionally fragmented country, passed down orally long before unification gave Italians a shared standard language. Collections like the nineteenth-century proverb anthologies, and even Pellegrino Artusi’s famous 1891 cookbook, helped fix many of these expressions in the national imagination.

Talk about it

  • Quali modi di dire di questa lezione ti sembrano più «calzanti» e quali, invece, già un po’ logori? _(Which idioms from this lesson strike you as most fitting, and which already feel a bit worn-out?)_
  • Esiste nella tua lingua un’espressione equivalente a «prendere due piccioni con una fava»? Usa la stessa immagine? _(Is there an equivalent in your language to “kill two birds with one stone”? Does it use the same image?)_
  • Perché, secondo te, tanti proverbi italiani nascono dal cibo (ciambelle, fave, pasta)? _(Why do you think so many Italian proverbs come from food — doughnuts, beans, dough?)_
  • In che situazioni un idioma rischia di risultare «fuori luogo»? Faresti un esempio? _(In what situations does an idiom risk sounding out of place? Could you give an example?)_
  • Quando impari una lingua, è meglio usare molti modi di dire o pochi ma ben azzeccati? Perché? _(When learning a language, is it better to use many idioms or few but well-chosen ones? Why?)_
