Grammar

Formal vs. informal greetings

Italian greetings change with the time of day and with how formal the situation is. Ciao is friendly and informal — use it with friends, family, and young people. Salve is a safe, neutral choice when you are unsure. Buongiorno and buonasera are polite and work in any formal setting.

tu vs. Lei: who am I talking to?

Italian has two ways to say you. Use tu with people you know well or who are your age or younger. Use Lei (often capitalized) to show respect to strangers, older people, and in shops or offices. The verb form changes with each.

FunctionInformal (tu)Formal (Lei)

Ask how they areCome stai?Come sta?
Ask where they’re fromDi dove sei?Di dov’è?

Saying your name and where you’re from

Use mi chiamo + name to introduce yourself, or simply sono + name. For origin, say sono di + city. Respond to an introduction with piacere (nice to meet you).

  • Mi chiamo Anna. — My name is Anna.
  • Sono Anna. — I’m Anna.
  • Sono di Firenze. — I’m from Florence.

Pronunciation tip

In Italian, ci and chi are different sounds. Ciao and piacere use a soft ch sound (like English cheese), while chiami and chiamo use a hard k sound — the h keeps the c hard before i.

Culture note

In Italy, greetings often come with a light kiss on each cheek (due baci) between friends and family, starting on the left. With strangers or in professional settings, a handshake and buongiorno or salve are more appropriate. The switch from buongiorno to buonasera can happen as early as the lunch break, especially in central and southern Italy.
