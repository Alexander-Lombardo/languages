Essere & Avere (to be / to have)

Dialogue

Anna

Ciao! Tu sei italiano?

Hi! Are you Italian?

Marco

Sì, sono di Roma. E tu?

Yes, I’m from Rome. And you?

Anna

Io sono spagnola, ma ho un’amica italiana.

I’m Spanish, but I have an Italian friend.

Marco

Quanti anni hai?

How old are you?

Anna

Ho ventidue anni. E ho molta fame!

I’m twenty-two. And I’m very hungry!

Marco

Anch’io ho fame. C’è un bar qui vicino.

Me too, I’m hungry. There’s a café nearby.

Anna

Perfetto. Ci sono anche dei panini?

Perfect. Are there sandwiches too?

Marco

Sì, ci sono panini e caffè. Andiamo!

Yes, there are sandwiches and coffee. Let’s go!

Grammar

Essere — the verb “to be”

Essere is irregular and one of the most useful verbs in Italian. Because the ending already shows the subject, the pronoun (io, tu, lui…) is usually dropped.

PronounEssereEnglish
iosonoI am
tuseiyou are (informal)
lui / lei / Leièhe / she is, you are (formal)
noisiamowe are
voisieteyou are (plural)
lorosonothey are

Note that sono means both I am and they are; context makes it clear. The form è always carries the grave accent to distinguish it from e (“and”).

Avere — the verb “to have”

Avere is also irregular. Its forms begin with a silent h, which is never pronounced — it only marks the verb in writing.

Idioms with avere

Where English uses to be, Italian often uses avere for physical states. Literally you “have” hunger, thirst, sleep, heat, cold, or a number of years.

  • avere fame — to be hungry (Ho fame. = I’m hungry.)
  • avere sete — to be thirsty
  • avere sonno — to be sleepy
  • avere caldo / freddo — to be hot / cold (a person)
  • avere … anni — to be … years old (Ho trent’anni. = I’m thirty.)

To ask someone’s age: Quanti anni hai? (informal) or Quanti anni ha? (formal).

C’è and ci sono — “there is / there are”

Use c’è (from ci + è) before a singular noun and ci sono before a plural noun.

  • C’è un bar qui vicino. — There’s a café nearby.
  • Ci sono due panini. — There are two sandwiches.

Pronunciation tip

The h in ho, hai, ha, hanno is completely silent — say “o, ai, a, anno.” It exists only to keep these verb forms distinct from the words o (or), ai (to the), a (to) and anno (year). Also remember that è (is) wears its accent so it doesn’t look like e (and).

Culture note

In Italy, the bar isn’t a pub — it’s a café where people stop for an espresso, a cappuccino or a quick panino. Standing at the counter (al banco) is usually cheaper than sitting at a table, and a morning coffee taken in seconds while chatting with the barista is a daily ritual all over the country.
