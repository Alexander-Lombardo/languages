Food & Ordering at the Bar

Dialogue

Cameriere

Buongiorno! Mi dica.

Good morning! What can I get you?

Anna

Vorrei un cappuccino e un cornetto, per favore.

I'd like a cappuccino and a croissant, please.

Cameriere

Certo. E per Lei, signore?

Of course. And for you, sir?

Luca

Per me un caffè e un po’ di acqua.

Cameriere

Cameriere

Anna

Cameriere

Grammar

Three polite ways to order

At the bar you almost never say “I want” (Voglio) — it sounds blunt. Use one of these instead, all followed simply by the item:

PhraseLiteralExample
Vorrei…I would likeVorrei un caffè.
Prendo…I'll take / havePrendo un cornetto.
Per me…For mePer me un tè.

Add per favore or per piacere (please) to be extra polite. Vorrei is the conditional of volere and is the softest, most common choice.

Asking the price & the bill

For one thing use Quanto costa? (how much does it cost?); for several use the plural Quanto costano?. To total everything up, Italians often say Quant’è? (how much is it all?). When you are ready to pay, ask for il conto: Il conto, per favore.

ItalianEnglish
Quanto costa il panino?How much is the sandwich?
Quanto costano i cornetti?How much are the croissants?
Quant’è in tutto?How much is it all together?
Il conto, per favore.The bill, please.

The partitive: some with un po’ di and del

To say some / a bit of something, you have two options. Un po’ di means a little / a bit of and never changes: un po’ di zucchero, un po’ di acqua. The other way is the partitive article, made from di + the, which agrees with the noun:

di +=Example
di + ildeldel pane (some bread)
di + lodellodello zucchero (some sugar)
di + l’dell’dell’acqua (some water)
di + ladelladella torta (some cake)
di + ideidei panini (some sandwiches)
di + ledelledelle paste (some pastries)

So Vorrei del tè and Vorrei un po’ di tè both mean “I'd like some tea.”

Pronunciation tip

The accent on caffè and tè: the grave accent marks the stress on the final syllable, so you push the voice to the end: kaf-FEH, TEH. Don't say CAF-feh. The same final-stress rule explains why perché and città are written with an accent.

The apostrophe in un po’: po’ is a shortened form of poco, so it takes an apostrophe, not an accent — write un po’ di, never un pò.

Culture note

In an Italian bar there are often two prices: cheaper if you drink your caffè standing al banco (at the counter) and more expensive if you sit at a table (al tavolo), where you pay for the service. A caffè means an espresso, and ordering a cappuccino after lunch or dinner is considered unusual — Italians drink it only in the morning. You usually pay at the till and many bars want you to order and pay before being served.
