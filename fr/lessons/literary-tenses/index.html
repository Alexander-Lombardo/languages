Literary Tenses (passé simple & subjonctif imparfait)

What you'll learn

Dialogue

Hélène

Tu lis encore ce vieux roman ? La langue ne te paraît pas trop datée ?

Are you still reading that old novel? Doesn't the language seem too dated to you?

Marc

Au contraire, j'adore. « Il entra, la salua, puis s'assit sans dire un mot. »

On the contrary, I love it. "He came in, greeted her, then sat down without saying a word."

Hélène

C'est du passé simple. On ne l'emploie plus qu'à l'écrit, surtout dans les récits.

That's the passé simple. It's only used in writing now, especially in narratives.

Marc

Justement. À l'oral je dirais « il est entré », mais le narrateur, lui, écrit « il entra ».

Exactly. Out loud I'd say "il est entré," but the narrator writes "il entra."

Hélène

Et là, regarde : « il eût voulu fuir ». Ça, c'est l'imparfait du subjonctif, presque disparu.

And there, look: "he would have liked to flee." That's the imperfect subjunctive, almost extinct.

Marc

On le reconnaît, mais personne ne le produit plus. Une plume d'autrefois !

We recognise it, but nobody produces it anymore. A pen from days gone by!

Grammar

Les temps littéraires — the literary tenses

Certain tenses live almost exclusively on the page. Your goal at C1 is to recognise them fluently while reading, and to produce the passé simple in the third person, since that is the one you will actually use if you ever write a story.

1. Le passé simple — the narrative past

In writing, the passé simple replaces the passé composé for completed, foregrounded actions in a story. Compare: spoken « Il est entré et il a parlé. » versus written « Il entra et parla. » Both mean the same thing; only the register differs. Narration is overwhelmingly in the third person (il / elle / ils / elles), so focus your production there.

2. Le passé antérieur (recognition)

This is the literary equivalent of the plus-que-parfait: passé simple of avoir/être + past participle. It marks an action completed just before another, usually after quand, lorsque, dès que, après que, aussitôt que.

« Lorsqu'il eut refermé les volets, un frisson le parcourut. » — "Once he had closed the shutters, a shiver ran through him." The eut refermé happens first; the parcourut follows.

3. L'imparfait du subjonctif (recognition only)

Built on the passé simple stem, it appears in older or very formal literature where the rule of concordance des temps once required a past subjunctive: qu'il parlât, qu'il fût, qu'il eût, qu'il vînt. Today, spoken and modern written French simply use the présent du subjonctif instead.

  • Old/literary: Il fallait qu'il partît.
  • Modern: Il fallait qu'il parte.

Watch for the circumflex (fût, eût, partît), which distinguishes these from the indicative passé simple. You need only to understand them, never to manufacture them in your own writing.

Pronunciation tip

The passé simple is a written tense, but you must still read it aloud convincingly. The third-person singular -er ending -a (parla) is an open "ah", clearly distinct from the future -era and from the present parle (silent ending). For -ir/-re verbs, finit and rendit end in a crisp "-ee" with the final consonant silent.

The circumflex forms carry no extra length in speech: fût sounds exactly like fut, and qu'il eût like qu'il eut. The accent is a spelling fossil that tells the reader "subjunctive," not a pronunciation cue — so when reading aloud, let context, not the vowel, signal the literary mood.

Reading: L'arrivée de l'étranger (extrait)

Le voyageur poussa la lourde porte de l'auberge et entra. Personne ne le reconnut, car nul ne l'avait jamais vu dans le village. Il ôta son manteau trempé, s'avança vers la cheminée et demanda une chambre. L'aubergiste, méfiant, le dévisagea longuement ; puis il prit la clé et la lui tendit sans un mot. L'étranger monta l'escalier, ouvrit la fenêtre et contempla la nuit. Au loin, les cloches sonnèrent minuit. Lorsqu'il eut refermé les volets, un frisson le parcourut : il sut alors qu'il ne repartirait pas le lendemain. Les villageois, eux, ne comprirent rien à cette visite. Le maire voulut l'interroger, mais l'homme ne répondit pas. Il resta trois jours, dit peu de mots, et disparut une nuit sans que personne ne s'en aperçût. On raconta longtemps son histoire au coin du feu, et chacun, à sa manière, inventa une fin que jamais nul ne put vérifier. Ainsi naquit, dans ce hameau oublié, une légende dont on parle encore.

Translation

The traveller pushed open the heavy door of the inn and went in. No one recognised him, for no one had ever seen him in the village. He took off his soaked coat, stepped toward the fireplace and asked for a room. The innkeeper, wary, stared at him at length; then he took the key and handed it to him without a word. The stranger climbed the stairs, opened the window and gazed at the night. In the distance, the bells struck midnight. When he had closed the shutters again, a shiver ran through him: he knew then that he would not leave the next day. The villagers, for their part, understood nothing of this visit. The mayor wanted to question him, but the man did not answer. He stayed three days, said few words, and vanished one night without anyone noticing. His story was told for a long time by the fireside, and each person, in their own way, invented an ending that no one could ever verify. Thus was born, in that forgotten hamlet, a legend still spoken of today.

Culture note

French schoolchildren still learn the passé simple, but almost no one speaks it. It survives in novels, fairy tales (« Il était une fois… le prince épousa la princesse »), history books, and a few set phrases. A French reader processes il vint, il fut, il prit effortlessly, yet would find them comically pompous in conversation — which is exactly the joke writers play when a character suddenly slips into the tense out loud.

The imparfait du subjonctif is even more of a museum piece. Its rarer forms (que nous chantassions, qu'il vous plût) are now a running gag in French humour, prized for their absurd-sounding endings. Knowing them won't help you order a coffee, but it unlocks the texture of classic authors like Flaubert, Hugo, and Maupassant — and lets you catch the wink when a modern writer deploys one on purpose.

Talk about it

  • Quel roman ou conte vous a le plus marqué, et pourquoi ? — Which novel or tale made the strongest impression on you, and why?
  • Préférez-vous les récits à la première ou à la troisième personne ? Justifiez. — Do you prefer first- or third-person narratives? Justify your answer.
  • Selon vous, pourquoi le passé simple a-t-il disparu de la langue parlée ? — In your opinion, why has the passé simple vanished from spoken French?
  • Racontez en quelques phrases une légende de votre région, au passé simple si vous l'osez. — Tell, in a few sentences, a legend from your region, in the passé simple if you dare.
  • Un dénouement ouvert ou une fin nette : que recherchez-vous dans une histoire ? — An open ending or a clear conclusion: what do you look for in a story?
