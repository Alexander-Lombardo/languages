Francophone Varieties (France, Québec, Africa)

Open the interactive lesson — free

What you'll learn

  • Understand and produce the Belgian and Swiss number system: septante (70), nonante (90), and Swiss huitante/octante (80), versus standard soixante-dix / quatre-vingt-dix.
  • Recognise the meal-name shift across varieties: déjeuner, dîner and souper in Belgium, Switzerland and Québec versus petit-déjeuner, déjeuner, dîner in France.
  • Master core Québécois vocabulary (un char, magasiner, une blonde / un chum, c'est correct, courriel, fin de semaine, bienvenue = you're welcome) and the strong preference for French over anglicisms (stationnement, courriel).
  • Identify a few Belgicismes and helvétismes (une drache, un GSM, une septantaine, le natel) and a handful of widely cited features of African French and its role as a lingua franca.
  • Discuss these as legitimate, equal varieties rather than "errors", and switch register and lexicon appropriately when speaking with Francophones from different regions.

Dialogue

Léa (France)

Tu viens d'arriver de Montréal ? Raconte, qu'est-ce qui t'a le plus surpris ?

You've just arrived from Montréal? Tell me, what surprised you the most?

Mathis (Québec)

Au début, le vocabulaire ! Là-bas je dis « mon char » pour ma voiture, et « magasiner » au lieu de « faire les courses ». Et le samedi, c'est « la fin de semaine », jamais « le week-end ».

At first, the vocabulary! Over there I say "mon char" for my car, and "magasiner" instead of "doing the shopping". And Saturday is "la fin de semaine", never "le week-end".

Léa (France)

On m'a aussi dit qu'au Québec « bienvenue » veut dire « de rien » quand on remercie quelqu'un.

I was also told that in Québec "bienvenue" means "you're welcome" when you thank someone.

Mathis (Québec)

Exactement. Et on tient beaucoup au français : on écrit « un courriel », pas « un mail », et on gare son char dans un « stationnement », pas dans un « parking ».

Exactly. And we're very attached to French: we write "un courriel", not "un mail", and we park the car in a "stationnement", not a "parking".

Noah (Belgique)

Chez nous, en Belgique, c'est surtout les chiffres qui vous déroutent : on dit septante pour 70 et nonante pour 90. Et quand il pleut très fort, on dit « il drache ».

Here in Belgium, it's mostly the numbers that throw you off: we say septante for 70 and nonante for 90. And when it pours, we say "il drache".

Sophie (Suisse)

En Suisse romande aussi : septante, nonante, et même huitante pour 80 dans plusieurs cantons. Et le soir, on ne dîne pas, on soupe.

In French-speaking Switzerland too: septante, nonante, and even huitante for 80 in several cantons. And in the evening, we don't "dîner", we "souper".

Léa (France)

Donc le repas du midi, le « déjeuner » en France, devient le « dîner » chez vous, et le repas du soir, le « souper ».

So the midday meal, "déjeuner" in France, becomes "dîner" for you, and the evening meal, "souper".

Mathis (Québec)

Voilà. Aucune de ces variétés n'est plus correcte qu'une autre — c'est simplement le génie de la francophonie : une langue, mille couleurs.

That's it. None of these varieties is more correct than another — it's simply the genius of the Francophone world: one language, a thousand colours.

Grammar

1. Belgium & Switzerland: counting differently

The standard French numbers soixante-dix (70) and quatre-vingt-dix (90) are arithmetical relics (60+10, 4×20+10). Belgium and French-speaking Switzerland keep the older, more transparent Latin-based forms:

71soixante et onzeseptante et unseptante et un
80quatre-vingtsquatre-vingtshuitante / quatre-vingts
90quatre-vingt-dixnonantenonante
92quatre-vingt-douzenonante-deuxnonante-deux

Note the nuances: septante and nonante are used in both Belgium and Switzerland. Belgium keeps quatre-vingts for 80; only Switzerland uses huitante (and the older, now rare octante), and even there it varies by canton — Geneva tends to say quatre-vingts. These forms are fully standard locally, not slang.

2. The shifting meal names

One of the clearest dividing lines in the Francophone world is what you call your meals. France made a historical shift that Belgium, Switzerland and Québec did not follow:

MealFranceBelgium · Switzerland · Québec
morning mealle petit-déjeunerle déjeuner
midday mealle déjeunerle dîner
evening mealle dînerle souper

So « On soupe à quelle heure ? » is perfectly normal in Brussels, Lausanne or Montréal, and there souper simply means "to have the evening meal", not a late-night snack as it can in France.

3. Québec: a French that defends itself

Québécois French combines old regional vocabulary with a deliberate, institutional resistance to English (driven by the Office québécois de la langue française). Paradoxically, everyday speech borrows freely from English, yet official and careful usage often prefers a homegrown French word where France happily uses the English one:

  • un courriel (coined in Québec from courrier électronique) where France says un mail / un e-mail.
  • le stationnement where France says le parking.
  • magasiner (and le magasinage) where France says faire du shopping / les courses.
  • la fin de semaine where France says le week-end.
  • bienvenue as a reply to merci (calqued on English "you're welcome"), where France says de rien / je vous en prie.

Other staples: un char (a car), une blonde / un chum (girlfriend / boyfriend), c'est correct (it's fine), tomber en amour (to fall in love, calqued on English). Tutoiement is far more widespread than in France: customers, colleagues and strangers say tu readily, and vous can sound stiff or distancing.

5. Africa and the wider Francophone world

The largest numbers of French speakers are now in Africa, where French is frequently a lingua franca alongside many local languages rather than a mother tongue. Each country develops its own coinages: for example, in parts of West and Central Africa one hears une essencerie (a petrol station) or un taximan (a taxi driver), and verbs may be created from local realities. The point at C2 is descriptive, not exhaustive: these are legitimate varieties, shaped by contact and need, exactly as Belgian, Swiss and Québécois French are. There is no single "pure" French sitting above the others — only a family of fully valid forms.

Pronunciation tip

Québécois pronunciation has two famous traits. Assibilation: t and d before the close vowels i and u turn into "ts" and "dz", so tu dis sounds like "tsu dzi" and petit like "petsi". Diphthongisation: long vowels glide, so père can sound like "paèr" and fête like "faèt". The vowels â and an are also more open and back than in France.

For the numbers, keep them clean and two-syllabled: septante ("sep-TAHNT"), nonante ("noh-NAHNT"), huitante ("wee-TAHNT") — the -ante ending nasalises just like in quarante and cinquante, so they slot naturally into the counting rhythm you already know.

Reading: Une langue, plusieurs mondes

On parle souvent du français comme s'il n'en existait qu'une seule version, celle de Paris. Pourtant, la francophonie rassemble près de trois cents millions de locuteurs répartis sur les cinq continents, et chaque région a façonné la langue à son image.

En Belgique et en Suisse romande, on compte autrement : septante pour soixante-dix, nonante pour quatre-vingt-dix, et, dans plusieurs cantons suisses, huitante pour quatre-vingts. Le repas du soir s'appelle souvent le souper. Au Québec, le français résiste avec fierté à la pression de l'anglais : on écrit un courriel, on gare son char dans un stationnement, on va magasiner la fin de semaine.

En Afrique, où le français sert souvent de langue commune entre des dizaines de langues locales, le vocabulaire s'enrichit sans cesse de créations propres à chaque pays. De Dakar à Kinshasa, on entend des tournures que Paris ignore.

Aucune de ces variétés n'est une faute : ce sont des français à part entière, vivants et légitimes. Comprendre la francophonie, c'est accepter que la norme parisienne n'est qu'une voix parmi beaucoup d'autres.

Translation

We often speak of French as if there were only one version, that of Paris. Yet the Francophone world brings together nearly three hundred million speakers across five continents, and each region has shaped the language in its own image.

In Belgium and French-speaking Switzerland, people count differently: septante for seventy, nonante for ninety, and, in several Swiss cantons, huitante for eighty. The evening meal is often called le souper. In Québec, French proudly resists the pressure of English: people write un courriel, park their car in a stationnement, and go shopping (magasiner) on the weekend.

In Africa, where French often serves as a common language among dozens of local tongues, the vocabulary is constantly enriched by coinages specific to each country. From Dakar to Kinshasa, you hear turns of phrase unknown in Paris.

None of these varieties is a mistake: they are full-fledged forms of French, living and legitimate. To understand the Francophone world is to accept that the Parisian norm is only one voice among many others.

Culture note

The word francophonie carries both a linguistic and a political meaning. With a small f it names everyone who speaks French; with a capital F, la Francophonie refers to the Organisation internationale de la Francophonie (OIF), an institution of dozens of member states and governments that promotes the language and cultural cooperation. The OIF holds that the future of French lies less in Paris than in Africa, which by mid-century is projected to hold the great majority of the world's French speakers.

Regional pride in French runs deep. Belgians and Swiss defend septante and nonante with a smile, and Québec has made language protection a matter of identity: the Charte de la langue française (Loi 101) shapes everything from shop signs to the workplace. For a C2 speaker, the lesson is one of humility — Parisian French is a prestige standard, but it is not "the" French. Mastery means hearing the difference between an error and a variety, and respecting the second.

Talk about it

  • Trouvez-vous le système septante / nonante plus logique que soixante-dix / quatre-vingt-dix ? Pourquoi ? — Do you find the septante / nonante system more logical than soixante-dix / quatre-vingt-dix? Why?
  • Le Québec lutte pour imposer « courriel » et « stationnement ». Cette résistance à l'anglais vous semble-t-elle utile ou vaine ? — Québec fights to impose "courriel" and "stationnement". Does this resistance to English seem useful or futile to you?
  • Existe-t-il, dans votre langue maternelle, des variétés régionales aussi marquées ? Donnez des exemples. — Are there, in your native language, regional varieties just as marked? Give examples.
  • Devrait-on enseigner aux apprenants une seule norme ou les exposer à plusieurs variétés du français ? — Should learners be taught a single norm or exposed to several varieties of French?
  • En quoi le rôle du français comme langue commune en Afrique change-t-il l'avenir de la francophonie ? — How does French's role as a common language in Africa change the future of the Francophone world?
