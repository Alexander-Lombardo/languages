What you'll learn

  • Use the analytical present ("le présent de narration" and the present of commentary) to comment on a literary text: "Dans ce roman, l'auteur dénonce…".
  • Identify and name the main figures of style: la métaphore, la comparaison, la personnification, l'hyperbole, l'anaphore, l'oxymore, la métonymie.
  • Handle the core vocabulary of literary analysis: le narrateur, la focalisation, l'intrigue, le registre, le champ lexical, une œuvre, un extrait, un personnage.
  • Recognise the passé simple as the tense of literary narration and distinguish it from the analytical present.
  • Situate a few major French authors and movements (Hugo, Baudelaire, Camus) and discuss a text with precision and nuance.

Dialogue

Inès

Tu as fini L'Étranger de Camus ? Qu'en penses-tu ?

Have you finished Camus's The Stranger? What do you think of it?

Théo

C'est troublant. Le narrateur raconte la mort de sa mère sans la moindre émotion ; la focalisation interne nous enferme dans son indifférence.

It's unsettling. The narrator recounts his mother's death without the slightest emotion; the internal point of view locks us inside his indifference.

Inès

Exactement. À travers ce personnage, Camus dénonce l'absurdité de l'existence et le poids des conventions sociales.

Exactly. Through this character, Camus exposes the absurdity of existence and the weight of social conventions.

Théo

Et le style est d'une sobriété frappante : des phrases courtes, presque aucune métaphore, comme si le monde avait perdu son sens.

And the style is strikingly spare: short sentences, almost no metaphor, as if the world had lost its meaning.

Inès

Le dénouement m'a bouleversée. Le registre devient soudain lyrique au moment où Meursault accepte sa condition.

The ending overwhelmed me. The register suddenly becomes lyrical at the moment Meursault accepts his fate.

Théo

C'est ce qui fait la force de l'œuvre : elle te dérange longtemps après la dernière page.

That's what makes the work so powerful: it disturbs you long after the last page.

Grammar

1. The analytical present: commenting on a text

To analyse literature in French you write in the present tense, even when discussing a centuries-old work. This is the present of commentary, the standard register of the commentaire de texte:

  • Dans ce roman, l'auteur dénonce l'injustice sociale.
  • Le narrateur adopte un point de vue interne et nous fait partager ses doutes.
  • Cette métaphore traduit le désespoir du personnage.

The closely related présent de narration is used inside a story to make past events feel vivid and immediate: Soudain, la porte s'ouvre et Valjean entre. Both share the same form; what changes is the purpose — to comment, or to dramatise.

2. Recognising the passé simple in narration (cf. Lesson 45)

Literary narration itself is usually told in the passé simple, the written tense of completed past actions. You rarely produce it in speech, but you must recognise it when reading:

  • Il entra, regarda la salle, puis s'assit. (entrer, regarder, s'asseoir)
  • Elle prit le livre et le lut jusqu'à l'aube. (prendre → prit, lire → lut)
  • Ils furent heureux ; ils eurent beaucoup d'enfants. (être → furent, avoir → eurent)

So you read the story in the passé simple, but you analyse it in the present: Hugo écrivit ce roman en 1862 (narration of his life) versus Hugo dénonce la misère (commentary).

Naming the devices precisely is the heart of analysis. Learn the term, the definition, and a French example:

4. The toolkit of analysis

  • le narrateur — who tells the story (≠ l'auteur, the real person who wrote it).
  • la focalisation — zéro (omniscient), interne (through one character), externe (a neutral camera).
  • l'intrigue — the chain of events; its end is le dénouement.
  • le registre — tragique, comique, pathétique, lyrique, épique, ironique.
  • le champ lexical — the network of words sharing a theme (e.g. le champ lexical de la mort).

Pronunciation tip

Several analytical words carry a silent ending or a tricky vowel. Œuvre begins with the rounded "eu" sound and the final -re is barely voiced: "UVR". In oxymore and métonymie, the y is pronounced like a French i ("ok-see-MOR", "may-toh-nee-MEE"). The h in hyperbole and anaphore is mute, so you say "ee-pehr-BOL" and "a-na-FOR".

When you read passé simple endings aloud, keep the -ut and -it crisp and silent at the end: il lut ("eel LEW"), il prit ("eel PREE"), ils furent ("eel FEWR"). The final t is never sounded.

Reading: Victor Hugo et la voix des humbles

Lorsqu'on lit Les Misérables, on est d'abord frappé par l'ampleur de l'œuvre. Victor Hugo n'y raconte pas seulement le destin de Jean Valjean ; à travers son intrigue, il dresse le portrait de toute une société et dénonce la misère qui broie les plus faibles.

Le narrateur, omniscient, se permet de longues digressions sur l'histoire, la justice ou l'argot. Cette focalisation lui donne le recul d'un témoin qui juge son époque. Hugo manie les figures de style avec une rare puissance : l'anaphore martèle ses indignations, l'hyperbole grandit ses héros, et la métaphore transforme Paris en un être vivant qui respire et souffre.

Le registre passe sans cesse du pathétique au sublime. Quand Hugo décrit Cosette enfant, le champ lexical de l'ombre et du froid serre le cœur du lecteur. Puis, dans le même chapitre, un éclat d'espoir illumine la page.

C'est sans doute pour cela que ce roman, publié en 1862, émeut encore : il ne se contente pas de raconter, il interpelle la conscience.

Translation

When you read Les Misérables, you are first struck by the sheer scope of the work. Victor Hugo does not merely tell the fate of Jean Valjean; through its plot, he paints the portrait of an entire society and denounces the poverty that crushes the weakest.

The narrator, omniscient, allows himself long digressions on history, justice, or slang. This point of view gives him the detachment of a witness judging his era. Hugo wields figures of speech with rare power: anaphora hammers home his outrage, hyperbole magnifies his heroes, and metaphor turns Paris into a living being that breathes and suffers.

The register shifts constantly from the pathetic to the sublime. When Hugo describes Cosette as a child, the lexical field of shadow and cold grips the reader's heart. Then, in the same chapter, a burst of hope lights up the page.

That is no doubt why this novel, published in 1862, still moves us: it does not settle for telling a story, it appeals to the conscience.

Culture note

The commentaire de texte and the dissertation are rites of passage in French education, drilled from the lycée through the dreaded baccalauréat de français at the end of the première year. Pupils learn to dissect a passage — naming its figures of style, its focalisation, its registre — with an almost surgical vocabulary. This shared training explains why even casual French conversations about books can suddenly turn remarkably technical.

The French literary canon is also bound to its movements: le classicisme of the 17th century (Molière, Racine), le romantisme of Hugo and Lamartine, the scandalous modernity of Baudelaire's Les Fleurs du mal, and the 20th-century existentialisme of Camus and Sartre. Knowing where an author stands in this lineage is, for the French, half of understanding the work itself.

Talk about it

  • Quelle œuvre littéraire vous a le plus marqué(e), et pourquoi ? — Which literary work has affected you the most, and why?
  • Préférez-vous un narrateur omniscient ou une focalisation interne ? Justifiez votre choix. — Do you prefer an omniscient narrator or an internal point of view? Justify your choice.
  • Selon vous, la littérature doit-elle dénoncer les injustices ou simplement divertir ? — In your view, should literature denounce injustice or simply entertain?
  • Quel registre vous touche le plus : le tragique, le comique ou le lyrique ? — Which register moves you most: tragic, comic, or lyrical?
  • Citez une figure de style que vous trouvez particulièrement efficace et expliquez son effet. — Name a figure of speech you find particularly effective and explain its effect.
